1.1What is a business?

The nature of business

Definition

A business is a decision making organization involved in the process of using inputs to produce goods and/or to provide services.

Businesses are organizations involved in the production of goods and/or the provision of services.

Definition

Inputs are the resources that a business uses in the production process, such as labour and raw materials. The process generates outputs (also known as products).

Definition

Production is the process of creating goods and/or services, adding value in the process.

Peter Drucker said that "there is only one valid definition of business purpose: to create a customer". A business does this by creating goods and services that meet the needs and wants of customers. The role of businesses is to combine human, physical and financial resources to create goods and services that satisfy the needs and wants of people, organizations and governments.

Goods and services

Definition

Goods are physical products produced and sold to customers, such as laptops, books, contact lenses, perfumes and children's toys.

Services are intangible products sold to customers, such as the services provided by airlines, restaurants, cinemas, banks, health and beauty spas, schools and hospitals.

  • The word product can mean both goods and services.
  • Goods are physical (smartphones, clothes, books, food). Services are intangible (haircuts, public transport, education, healthcare).
  • With a service, the service itself cannot be touched, but the results or experiences are real. Examples are healthcare, transportation, dining, sports (recreation), legal advice, financial guidance and education.
  • Businesses can also sell goods and services to other organizations, for example freight transportation, distribution and insurance.

Needs and wants

Definition

Needs are the basic necessities that a person must have to survive, including food, water, warmth, shelter and clothing.

Wants are people's desires, i.e. the things they would like to have, such as new clothes, smartphones, overseas holidays and jewellery.

Examples from the textbook: a larger home, a new smartphone, an overseas holiday or a birthday cake are wants, not needs.

Customers and consumers

Definition

Customers are the people or organizations that buy the product.

Consumers are the people or organizations that actually use a product.

They can be the same person (someone who buys and eats a meal). They can also be different: parents (customers) buy presents for their children (consumers).

Common mistake

Students often use "customer" and "consumer" as if they mean the same thing. Always ask: who pays, and who uses?

Adding value

Definition

Adding value is the practice of producing a good or service that is worth more than the cost of the resources used in the production process.

  • The purpose of business activity is to generate added value.
  • Added value exists when there is a positive difference between the selling price and the cost of producing the good or service.
  • It exists when products appeal to customers, so they are willing to pay a higher price.
Worked example (illustration)

A bakery uses ingredients and energy that cost $3 to make a birthday cake. It sells the cake for $12.

Added value = selling price − cost of production = $12 − $3 = $9.

Entrepreneurs and entrepreneurship

Definition

Entrepreneurs are the people who manage, organize and plan the resources needed for business activity in pursuit of organizational objectives. They are risk takers who exploit business opportunities in return for profits.

Entrepreneurship refers to the collective knowledge, skills and experiences of entrepreneurs.

  • An entrepreneur plans, organizes and manages a business and its operations, taking on financial risks. The word was coined by Richard Cantillon (1680 to 1734), an Irish French economist.
  • Entrepreneurship also describes the trait of business leaders who are distinctive in temperament, attitude and outlook and who drive the business towards its goals.
  • Successful entrepreneurs tend to be creative, innovative and highly passionate. They search for and exploit opportunities by forecasting and/or responding to changes in the marketplace.
Case study: Steve Jobs

Steve Jobs (1955 to 2011) did not graduate from university because his adopted parents could not afford the fees. He took the risk of setting up Apple in 1976 with Steve Wozniak and Ronald Wayne. Wayne sold his share within weeks for just $2,300. Jobs went on to reinvent computing, music and mobile phones. Apple became the highest valued company in the world two months before he died, and Forbes estimated his wealth at over $8.3 billion.

Point it proves: entrepreneurs take big financial risks, and the reward can be very large.

Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors

Businesses can be classified by the stage of production (the chain of production) they are engaged in.

Definition

Primary sector refers to businesses involved in the cultivation or extraction of natural resources, such as farming, mining, quarrying, fishing, oil exploration and forestry.

Secondary sector refers to businesses concerned with the construction and manufacturing of products.

Tertiary sector refers to businesses involved with the provision of services to customers.

Quaternary sector is a sub category of the tertiary sector, where businesses are involved in intellectual and knowledge based activities that generate and share information, such as research organizations.

How the sector mix changes

  • As countries achieve sustained economic growth and development, they rely less on the primary sector for jobs and national output, partly because there is little added value in primary production. For example, low income countries can only sell tea leaves and coffee beans at low prices.
  • The primary sector is still important to every country. Less than 4% of France's national output comes from it, yet France benefits hugely from agricultural exports.
  • A restaurant is a service even though the chef changes physical ingredients. The focus is on the people providing the service (the chef and waiting staff), not on the tangible product.
  • Apple and Samsung see themselves as being in the services sector. They outsource production of smartphones and hardware to manufacturers such as Foxconn. There is more added value in the tertiary and quaternary sectors: instead of one off payments from selling equipment, Apple gets a flow of revenue from after sales services such as maintenance and support.

The chain of production

Definition

The chain of production tracks the stages of an item's production, from the extraction of raw materials all the way through to it being delivered to the consumer. It links the four sectors.

The sectors are interdependent, because each relies on the others to exist.

  • Crude oil would not be extracted if no one needed oil refining. Refiners would not exist without customers such as motorists and airlines (tertiary).
  • Secondary sector firms rely on suppliers for stocks so that they meet production targets.
  • All businesses need energy, ICT facilities, manufactured producer goods, financial services and management consultants.

Challenges for starting a business

Around 20 to 25 per cent of new businesses fail to survive their first year. A new business must deal with challenges at once, before they threaten its survival. The textbook lists these:

ChallengeWhat the problem is
Lack of financeBusinesses need money to buy fixed assets (premises, buildings, machinery, equipment). Start ups often cannot get enough funding. Borrowing may be too little, or high interest charges hurt cash flow. New sole traders often remortgage their homes, giving the lender more collateral (financial security in case the loan is not repaid).
Unestablished customer baseAttracting customers and building a broad, loyal customer base is hard, especially when strong competitors already exist. Loyalty takes a long time and may need marketing know how and large amounts of money.
Cash flow problemsFinancing working capital (money available for the daily running of the business) is hard. Stock (raw materials, semi finished or finished goods) cannot easily be turned into cash. Customers may demand a long credit period (usually 30 to 60 days), so cash arrives late, while wages, rent, utility bills, taxes and loan interest still have to be paid.
Marketing problemsFailing to meet customer needs leads to poor sales and low profit. New firms may lack the expertise to supply the right product to the right customers at the right price. A key to success is to find a niche (gap) in the market and fill it. Amazon saw the Internet as a way to distribute books and other products in the 1990s. easyJet and Ryanair spotted the niche for no frills (budget) air travel.
People management problemsStart ups may not know how to hire staff with the right skills. This causes poor labour productivity, retraining or rehiring, which is costly and slow. They may not know the best organizational structure or staff motivation methods.
Production problemsHard to forecast demand. Overproduction causes stockpiling, wastage and higher costs. Underproduction causes unhappy customers and lost sales.
LegalitiesMust follow all laws: business registration, insurance for staff and buildings, consumer protection laws, and intellectual property rules (copyrights, patents, trademarks). The paperwork is cumbersome, slow and expensive. Mistakes can mean compensation or financial penalties, which hurt weak cash flow.
High production costsHigh set up costs (Box 1.2) and running costs: capital equipment, machinery, stock, rent, advertising, insurance. Small firms cannot enjoy economies of scale, so their average costs are higher. Large firms get supplier discounts for bulk buying and borrow at lower interest because of their size and collateral.
Poor locationA dilemma: busy areas offer the most customers but cost the most. Fixed costs (rent, mortgage) are a big share of total costs. Start ups want to reach break even fast by keeping fixed costs low, so many begin from home (which also has some tax advantages). Jeff Bezos started Amazon in his garage. This does not work where location is key to survival.
External influencesAll businesses face exogenous shocks, such as a global financial crisis or a pandemic. Larger, established firms have more resources to cope, so new businesses are more vulnerable and more likely to fail.

Culture and business, and the key concepts

  • Reasons for setting up a business vary by country and culture. Singapore, Hong Kong and New Zealand need only a few legal steps, which encourages an entrepreneurial culture where creative and innovative businesses thrive. In Chad, Libya and Myanmar a risk taking entrepreneurial culture is less obvious, so there is little change and sustainability is more of an issue.
  • The nature of business activity is to satisfy customers' needs and desires while meeting the organization's own objectives, such as profit, growth and corporate social responsibility.
  • In the long run all businesses must make a profit or surplus to survive. For profit and not for profit organizations go about this in similar and different ways (see 1.2).
  • Change, ethics and creativity are central to the sustainability of businesses.
  • Business activity is the process of turning inputs (land, labour, capital and enterprise) into outputs (finished goods and services) to meet the needs and wants of different customers. All businesses try to add value as part of a sustainable business strategy.
Case study: Paul Buchheit and Gmail

Paul Buchheit, a Google employee, is credited as creator of Gmail. In 2001 he programmed the first working version in one day and was lead developer. He left Google in 2006, worked at Facebook until 2010, and won The Economist Innovation Award in 2011 for computing and telecommunications.

Point it proves: creativity and the ability to adapt to change are crucial to compete in business.

1.2Types of business entities

The private sector and the public sector

Definition

The private sector is the part of the economy run by private individuals and businesses, rather than by the government, such as sole traders, partnerships, privately held companies and publicly held companies.

The public sector is the part of the economy controlled by the government. Examples include state healthcare and education services, the emergency services, social housing and national defence.

Case study: Australia Post

Established in 1809, it is a state owned enterprise providing postal services. It employs over 35,000 people across more than 4,330 retail outlets, with revenues above AUD 7.5bn (about USD 5.45bn).

Reasons for public sector business activity

  • To make sure everyone can use basic services: education, health care, museums, public parks, public libraries.
  • To avoid wasteful competition. The government can reach huge economies of scale (cost savings from operating on a large scale) in services such as postal services and national defence.
  • To create employment. Governments are large employers of teachers, doctors and nurses.
  • To stabilise the economy. Many private banks were nationalised (bought by the government) in the 2008 global credit crisis to prevent more financial turmoil. Government action in the COVID-19 pandemic also helped stabilise economies.
  • To protect citizens and businesses through the police and courts, which run the law and order system.

Types of profit based organizations

A business can only survive in the long term if it is profitable. Profit based entities differ in ownership and control (you do not have to run a business just because you own it), in how they raise finance and in how profits are distributed. The four main types are sole traders, partnerships, privately held companies and publicly held companies.

(i) Sole traders

Definition

A sole trader is a self-employed person who runs the business on his/her own. This means s/he has exclusive responsibility for its success (profits) or failure (unlimited liability).

  • Also called a sole proprietor. The owner runs and controls the business. It is the most common type of business ownership in the world.
  • The business is unincorporated: the owner and the business are the same legal entity. If the business collapses, the owner may lose personal possessions.
  • Sole traders may work alone or employ others. They are often small family run businesses, set up with a relatively small amount of capital, usually from personal savings and borrowing.
  • Examples: self employed decorators, plumbers, mechanics, restaurateurs, private tutors, freelance photographers.
Examples of businesses that began as sole traders
  • Chanel: started by Coco Chanel in 1910, now global.
  • Marks & Spencer: set up in 1904 by Michael Marks and Thomas Spencer, who first operated as sole traders.
  • Tesco: started in 1919 as a sole proprietorship by Jack Cohen.
  • Walmart: in 1945 Sam Walton started with personal savings of $5,000 and a $20,000 loan from his father in law. It became the world's largest retailer.
  • eBay: created in 1995 by Pierre Omidyar as a sole proprietorship (prototype "Auction Web") after his fiancee had trouble selling collectibles such as PEZ candy dispensers. In 1998 eBay became a publicly held company, making him a billionaire at 31.

(ii) Partnerships

Definition

Partnerships are a type of private sector business entity owned by 2 to 20 people (known as partners). They share the responsibilities and burdens of running and owning the business.

A deed of partnership is the legal contract signed by the owners of a partnership. The formal deeds specify the name and responsibilities of each partner and their proportion of any profits or losses.

  • A partnership is a for profit private sector business owned by two or more persons. For ordinary partnerships the maximum is 20 owners (it varies by country). Exceptions include professions such as solicitors and accountants, where issuing shares is prohibited.
  • Like sole traders, partnerships are financed mainly from the owners' personal funds, but partners can pool funds to raise more.
  • Silent (sleeping) partners invest money but do not take part in running the business. They are entitled to a share of the profits.
  • Partnerships are unincorporated. At least one owner must have unlimited liability, but typically all partners share it.
  • A legal agreement is not required but most partnerships make one. Without a contract, profits or losses are shared equally and partners have the same rights in running the business.
A deed of partnership is likely to include
  • The amount of finance each partner contributes.
  • The roles, obligations and responsibilities of each partner.
  • How profits or losses are shared.
  • Conditions for introducing new partners.
  • Clauses for the withdrawal of a partner.

Why unlimited liability exists

It stops sole traders and partners making careless or irresponsible decisions, because it makes private individuals accountable for their actions. The downside is that the risk of losing private property can stop them from taking risks, so they choose safe decisions instead.

Definition

Unlimited liability is a feature of sole traders and ordinary partnerships who are legally liable for all monies owed to their creditors, even if this means they have to sell their personal possessions to pay their debts.

(iii) Privately held companies

Definition

A company (or corporation) refers to a limited liability business that is owned by shareholders. A certificate of incorporation gives the company a separate legal identity from its owners.

Incorporation means there is a legal difference between the owners of a company and the business itself. This ensures the owners are protected by limited liability.

Limited liability is a restriction on the amount of money that owners of a company can lose if the business goes bankrupt, i.e. shareholders cannot lose more than the amount they invested in the company.

A privately held company is a business owned by shareholders with limited liability but whose shares cannot be bought by or sold to the general public on a Stock Exchange.

  • Shareholders are individuals or other businesses that have invested money to provide share capital. Companies are sometimes called joint stock companies because the shares ("stock") are jointly held by many owners.
  • Because a company is a separate legal entity, it has its own legal rights and duties. For example, the company, not the owners, takes people who break copyright and patent laws to court.
  • Shareholders' personal belongings are safe if the company goes bankrupt or into liquidation. The most they can lose is the value of their investment. This protects investors.
  • A Board of Directors (BOD) is elected by shareholders to run the company for them. Directors are chosen for skills and expertise. The BOD is held responsible for running the company but is accountable to the shareholders.
  • Usually one share equals one vote. In reality individual shareholders have little say because large institutional investors and directors hold most shares.
  • Shares are sold to family members and friends. They cannot be bought or sold without prior agreement from the BOD, so the directors keep control. Many are family businesses. The name often ends in "Limited" or "Ltd", though this varies by country.
  • Setting up a company can be complicated and costly, e.g. there are rules for shares to be sold on a stock exchange.
Definition

A stock exchange is a marketplace for trading stocks and shares of publicly held companies (or public limited companies). Examples include the London Stock Exchange (LSE) and the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE).

Exam tip: business or company?

Do not use "business" and "company" as the same word. Companies are owned by shareholders, so there are only two types of company: privately held and publicly held. "Business" also includes sole traders and partnerships. All companies are businesses but not all businesses are companies.

Annual General Meeting (AGM)

All companies must hold an AGM so owners can have a say (vote). The three main processes:

  • Shareholders vote on resolutions (promises or declarations) and on the re election (sometimes election) of the Board of Directors.
  • Shareholders ask questions to the CEO, directors and chairperson.
  • Shareholders approve the previous year's financial accounts after directors present the annual report on financial performance.

(iv) Publicly held companies

Definition

A publicly held company is an incorporated limited liability business that allows shareholders to buy and sell shares in the company via a public Stock Exchange.

An initial public offering (IPO) occurs when a business sells all or part of its business to shareholders on a public stock exchange for the first time. This changes its legal status to a publicly held company.

  • It is like a privately held company, but it can advertise and sell shares to the general public. It often has "PLC" after its name.
  • Flotation is when a publicly held company first sells all or part of its business to outside investors. The IPO makes the company listed (registered) on a stock exchange. Flotation creates extra finance.
  • IPO examples: Agricultural Bank of China raised $22.1 billion in 2010; Alibaba raised $25 billion on the NYSE in 2014; Saudi Aramco raised $25.6 billion on the Tadawul Saudi Stock Exchange in 2019 (a record).
  • Largest shareholders are usually institutional and commercial investors (companies owning shares in other companies). Porsche is the majority shareholder of Volkswagen, which owns a majority of Audi, Bentley, Bugatti, Lamborghini, SEAT and Skoda.
Case study: Google

On 19 August 2004 Google Inc. floated on the NASDAQ, raising $2.7bn. Shares floated at $85 and reached over $600 by 2014 (up over 605% in ten years). In November 2021 the price peaked at $3,037, a rise of 3,473% since the IPO.

Common mistakes
  • It is wrong to say privately held companies are smaller than publicly held ones. IKEA, LEGO, Huawei, PricewaterhouseCoopers and Mars are market leaders.
  • Do not confuse a publicly held company with a public corporation. The first is a limited liability company in the private sector that offers shares to the public. The second is owned and run by the government for the public.

Risks for shareholders even with limited liability: an unprofitable company cannot pay dividends, and the share price is likely to fall (negative capital growth). Shareholders also trust the BOD and senior managers, but the interests of directors, managers and shareholders may conflict. For example, better pay and conditions for workers can reduce the funds available for dividends.

Social enterprises

Definition

Social enterprises are revenue generating businesses with social objectives at the core of their operations. They can be for profit or non profit business entities, but all profits or surpluses must be reinvested for that social purpose rather than distributed to shareholders and owners.

  • Commercial for profit businesses return a profit to their owners. For profit social enterprises return a surplus for social gain instead of personal gain.
  • All social enterprises have two main goals: (1) to achieve social objectives and (2) to earn revenue in excess of costs. Social entrepreneurs cannot do good if the business is not financially healthy. Without a surplus or profit there is no business for the greater good.
  • Main benefits: they use any financial surplus to benefit others in society; they create jobs and improve local communities; and they are run in a transparent way, giving tangible benefits and a "clean corporate conscience".
  • Social entrepreneurs are those who use businesses to solve social issues.
  • There are three main types of for profit social enterprises: private sector companies, public sector companies and cooperatives.
Case study: Grameen Bank

In 1983 social entrepreneur Muhammad Yunus set up the Grameen Bank in Dhaka, Bangladesh, the "bank for the poor". It gives small loans (microfinance) to people in poverty so they can become financially self sufficient, and does not require collateral. Over 97% of borrowers are women. It has assets above $2.8bn and over 20,000 workers. Yunus won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 and the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2009.

(i) Private sector companies (for profit social enterprises)

  • They work like normal for profit businesses, but they reinvest or donate the surplus to create positive social change. They use ethical business practices and earn revenue in socially responsible ways.
  • Unlike charities that rely on donations, they must earn a surplus to survive. The surplus also lets them expand their activities and outreach.
  • They produce goods or services like commercial firms and compete with other businesses in the same market. They can be private or publicly held companies, or cooperatives.
Triple bottom line

Both private and public sector for profit social enterprises have three broad aims, the triple bottom line, an accounting framework coined by John Elkington in 1994:

  • Economic aims: earn a profit (surplus) and reinvest it back into the business for societal benefit.
  • Social aims: benefit people in society, such as local jobs and support for less advantaged members of society.
  • Environmental aims: protect the planet by operating in environmentally friendly and sustainable ways.

(ii) Public sector companies (for profit social enterprises)

  • State owned enterprises run in a commercial way. Formed by the government through legal means and regulated, as they do commercial business for financial gain.
  • Owned wholly or partly by the government. They help raise government revenue yet provide essential services that might be inefficient or undesirable if left only to the private sector.
  • Examples: some national airlines, airport authorities, transport operators, telecommunications companies and postal services.
Examples of public sector companies
  • Australia Post: over 4,330 outlets across Australia.
  • Egyptian National Railways: Egypt's national railway, run by the state owned Egyptian Railway Authority.
  • Ontario Niagara Parks Commission: manages the Canadian side of Niagara Falls, focusing on reformed land use and sustainable tourism.
  • Television Nacional de Chile (TVN): leading Chilean public service broadcaster, founded in 1969.
  • Temasek Holdings: Singaporean state owned enterprise owning 56% of Singapore Airlines.
  • China Mobile: the world's largest mobile phone operator (over 945.5 million subscribers), founded 1997. The Chinese government owns 74.25%. It is listed on the NYSE and the Hong Kong Stock Exchange.
  • Hong Kong Disneyland: owned jointly by the Hong Kong government (majority shareholder) and Walt Disney Company. Opened 12 September 2005, created 36,000 jobs, and made no profit until 2012 (net profit HK$109m).

(iii) Cooperatives

Definition

Cooperatives are for profit social enterprises set up, owned and run by their members, who might be employees and/or customers.

  • Common goal: create value for members by operating in a socially responsible way. Every employee member has a vote, and profits are shared between members.
  • Found in retailing, financial services, childcare, housing associations and agriculture. Canada has over 9,000 cooperatives with over 18 million members. The UK's Cooperative Group is the world's largest consumer owned business with over 4.5 million members.
  • They follow the guidelines and principles of the International Cooperative Alliance (ICA), founded in 1895.
  • As cooperatives operate in the private sector, they are an example of private sector for profit social enterprises.

Non profit social enterprises

  • Run in a commercial like way, but profit is not the main goal. They use the surplus to achieve social goals instead of paying dividends to owners or shareholders.
  • Examples: public libraries, state schools, museums, government hospitals, social services. Well known ones: Habitat for Humanity, Oxfam, the Red Cross, the World Wide Fund for Nature.
  • The main type is the non governmental organization (NGO), including international charities.
Exam tip: "non profit"

"Non profit" does not mean the organization does not try to earn a profit or surplus. It means the money must be retained in the organization for its survival and growth. Profit is the reward given to owners or investors for risking their money. Non profit organizations (NPOs) keep the surplus. Many private fee paying schools are NPOs because surpluses are reinvested in facilities and teaching.

Non governmental organizations (NGOs)

Definition

Non governmental organizations (NGOs) are private sector not for profit social enterprises that operate for the benefit of others rather than primarily aiming to earn a profit, such as Oxfam and Friends of the Earth.

The United Nations defines NGOs as "private organizations that pursue activities to relieve suffering, promote the interest of the poor, protect the environment, provide basic social services or undertake community development".

  • The term was coined in Article 71 of the UN Charter (1945). An NGO (sometimes a private voluntary organization, PVO) can be any organization that (1) is independent of the government or direct public sector influence and (2) is not for profit.
  • Examples: Medecins Sans Frontieres (Doctors Without Borders), Friends of the Earth. Many are very large: Oxfam (poverty), Greenpeace (environment), Amnesty International (human rights), UNICEF (children's welfare).
  • They are run by senior managers and trustees, similar to a company's board of directors.

Choosing a business entity, and the key concepts

Factors that affect the strategic choice of business entity:

  • Amount of finance: sole traders and partnerships need less start up capital than a publicly held company. A change in legal status usually needs more finance.
  • Size: the larger and more complex the operations, the more likely a limited liability company. Sole traders find a large workforce or tall hierarchy unnecessary or unaffordable.
  • Limited liability: owners who want to protect personal possessions may choose a company.
  • Degree of ownership and control: owners who want to keep control may stay small as sole traders or privately held companies.
  • Nature of business activity: type and scale matter. Mainstream aircraft and motor vehicle makers rely on outside finance, so are likely to be publicly held companies.
  • Change: as a business grows and evolves (see 1.5) it may need more finance and people, so its legal status is likely to change.

Vision statements and mission statements

Definition

A vision statement is an organization's long term aspirations, i.e. where the business ultimately wants to be.

A mission statement refers to the declaration of an organization's overall purpose. It forms the foundation for setting the objectives of a business.

  • Having a vision means having an image of an ideal situation in the future. A vision statement outlines aspirations (where the organization wants to be) in the distant future and relates to the attainment of success, i.e. what success would look like. Example: Adidas, "To be the leading sports brand in the world". Martin Luther King Jr's "I have a Dream" speech (28 August 1963) shows how a clear vision can bring inspirational change.
  • Having a mission means having a clear purpose. A mission statement is usually a simple declaration of the underlying purpose of an organization's existence and its core values. Example for a school: "the provision of wide opportunities and achievement for all".
  • Unlike business objectives, a mission statement has no distinct time frame and tends to be qualitative rather than quantitative.
  • A well produced mission statement is clearly defined and realistically achievable. It gives a sense of direction, guides decision making and unifies people and corporate cultures to achieve the vision.

  • Even good statements may not be supported by everyone (e.g. part time or temporary staff). Changing people's beliefs and behaviour can take a long time.

Business objectives

Definition

Objectives specify what an organization strives to achieve. They are the goals of an organization, such as growth, profit, protecting shareholder value and ethical objectives.

Objectives are generally specific and quantifiable (measurable) and are set in line with the mission statement. Without clear objectives an organization has no sense of direction, like getting into a taxi without knowing where you want to go. Objectives matter for three reasons:

  • To motivate: they inspire managers and employees to reach a common goal, unifying and motivating the workforce. They also make managers think strategically (plan for long term success).
  • To direct: they give an agreed, clear focus for all individuals and departments. They are the foundation for decisions and are used to devise business strategies.
  • To measure and control: they set the parameters for business activity, so they are the basis for measuring and controlling performance of the whole business.

The syllabus lists four common objectives: (i) growth, (ii) profit, (iii) protecting shareholder value, (iv) ethical objectives.

(i) Growth

  • Growth is usually measured by an increase in sales revenue or in market share.
  • Market share is the firm's sales revenue expressed as a percentage of the industry's total sales revenue.
  • Growth is essential for survival in an ever changing, competitive world, especially with the risk of mergers and takeovers (see 1.5). Failing to grow may reduce competitiveness and threaten sustainability.
Worked example: market share

A firm has sales revenue of $20m. The whole industry has sales of $200m.

Market share = (firm's sales revenue ÷ industry's total sales revenue) × 100 = ($20m ÷ $200m) × 100 = 10%.

(ii) Profit

  • Traditionally the main objective of most private sector organizations is to maximize profits.
  • Profit gives an incentive for entrepreneurs to take risks in setting up and running a business.
  • For incorporated businesses, part of the profits (called dividends) is distributed to shareholders.
  • Without a profit motive, owners and investors find it hard to justify the business existing.
  • Milton Friedman (1912 to 2006, Nobel Prize in Economics 1976): "there is one and only one social responsibility of business: to use its resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits."

(iii) Protecting shareholder value

  • This objective is about generating long term value for shareholders (owners). It means earning a profitable return for shareholders in a sustainable way.
  • Most shareholders do not run the company day to day, so they rely on directors, managers and employees to protect their interests.
  • The board of directors is ultimately and directly responsible for protecting and managing shareholders' interests. Shareholder value is the result of the business strategy the board follows.

(iv) Ethical objectives

Definition

Ethics are the moral principles that guide decision making and business strategy. Morals are concerned with what is considered to be right or wrong, from society's point of view.

Ethical objectives are organizational goals based on moral guidelines, determined by the business and/or society, which direct and determine decision making.

An ethical code of practice is the documented beliefs and philosophies of an organization, so that people know what is considered acceptable or not acceptable within the organization.

  • Examples: direct marketing aimed at children is banned in many countries as unethical. Health and safety laws help stop the exploitation of employees.
  • Business ethics are the actions an organization takes that are considered morally correct. An ethical business acts morally towards its workers, customers, shareholders and the natural environment. For example, an ethical employer gives enough rest breaks.
  • Ethical objectives in line with circular business models (Chapter 51) include: disposing of waste in an environmentally safe way; fairer conditions of trade with low income countries; more recycling; reducing pollution with more environmentally friendly technologies and production processes.
  • An increasing number of businesses adopt an ethical code of practice (code of conduct) and publish it in annual reports. It gives guidelines on expected behaviour. Values differ between people and businesses, so a code of ethics (such as personal integrity and consideration for others) gives consistency in the workplace.
Box 3.2: Examples of unethical business behaviour
  • Financial dishonesty: mismanaging finances, e.g. deliberately misrepresenting the accounts (also illegal). Moral issues also arise with extravagant business expenses by directors (unethical but not illegal).
  • Environmental neglect and damage: business activity harming the natural environment, e.g. pollution and depletion of non renewable resources.
  • Exploitation of workers: deliberately neglecting employee welfare. Many multinationals are criticised for poor pay and conditions in low income countries.
  • Exploitation of suppliers: large businesses forcing suppliers to cut prices. Controversial especially in low income countries, or when savings are not passed to consumers as lower prices.
  • Exploitation of consumers: knowingly selling products with harmful side effects (tobacco, alcohol, gambling services), or firms with few competitors abusing market power by charging excessively high prices.

Strategic and tactical objectives

Definition

Strategic objectives are the longer term goals of a business, such as profit maximization, growth, market standing and increased market share.

Strategies are the various plans of action that businesses use to achieve their targets. They are the long term plans of the organization as a whole.

Tactical objectives are short term goals that affect a unit of the organization. They are specific goals that guide the daily functioning of certain departments or operations.

Tactics are the short term plans of action that businesses use to achieve their objectives.

Strategy and tactics have matching purposes: how a business intends to get to where it wants to be. Once short and long term goals are set, the business chooses the best methods to reach them.

Tactical objectives

  • Survival: new businesses face problems such as little brand recognition, a small customer base and intense rivalry. Established firms also need to survive in a recession (2008 financial crisis, COVID-19). A takeover target may also make its own survival a key objective.
  • Sales revenue maximization: new firms try to maximize sales revenue to establish themselves. Sales staff and agents (insurance, real estate) like it because their earnings depend on sales value. But sales revenue is not profit (the surplus left after all costs). A firm with high sales but low or no profit will struggle in the long run.

Strategic objectives (examples)

  • Market standing: how much presence a business has in its industry. Microsoft (market leader in software), Walmart (world's largest retailer), Toyota (world's largest car producer), Apple (innovative products and designs), The Body Shop (socially responsible). Good standing brings competitive advantages.
  • Image and reputation: a bad image, perhaps from the media, can turn customers away. Businesses improve customer service, facilities and after sales care. A positive image motivates employees, helps attract and keep high calibre staff, and suppliers prefer reputable firms.
  • Market share: sales revenue as a percentage of industry sales. The higher it is, the greater the market dominance and ability to compete.
  • In practice a business has a combination of strategic objectives, which change over time. For example, survival can be a key strategic objective when a firm faces a hostile takeover.
  • Organizational culture (Chapter 11) and whether the business is in the private or public sector also affect its objectives.
  • Objectives can conflict. For example, employees want better pay and conditions, which may cut profits in the short term. Managers must still base strategy on achieving the objectives.

Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

Definition

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is the conscientious consideration of ethical and environmental practice related to business activity. A business that adopts CSR acts morally towards all of its various stakeholder groups and the well being of society as a whole.

  • Examples: McDonald's employees go on litter patrol near restaurants and join local fundraising such as charity runs. Lenovo commits 1% of pre tax income to good causes. During COVID-19, Zoom's CEO Eric Yuan gave schools the premium version of the software free.
  • Media exposure means large multinationals are expected to give some profits to charity. Customers are careful about buying from socially irresponsible firms. Investors avoid unethical firms (e.g. those using child labour). Pressure groups and awareness make climate change and environmental damage big concerns, so businesses change objectives to reflect their role in protecting the planet. Polluting firms risk a poor corporate image with serious consequences.
  • A good reputation (how others view the organization) can give an important competitive advantage. According to Fortune, the top 500 US firms donate over 2% of post tax profits to charity. This could be a real wish to act responsibly or fear of bad publicity. Compliance costs add to expenses. For employees and investors to support CSR, they must believe it is in their interest too.

CSR is subjective

  • What is "right" or "wrong" depends largely on public opinion, so attitudes change over time. Advertising aimed at young children was once acceptable but may not be now. Environmental protection was not a major issue before the 1980s.
  • It differs by country: some do not see a minimum wage as needed, others use it to stop unethically low pay. Advertising tobacco is banned in many countries but not all. Serbia, Bangladesh and Russia are huge tobacco consumers, so cigarette advertising is less strict than in the UK and Singapore. Australia and Canada treat environmental concern as a key CSR indicator, while India and China place less emphasis on it.
  • This makes CSR harder for businesses that operate in several countries.

Whether a business acts in a socially responsible way depends on these interrelated factors:

  • Societal expectations (public awareness and concern about CSR).
  • Exposure and pressure from the mass media.
  • Experience: it often takes a crisis (Chapter 41) or bad experience to make firms pay attention to CSR.
  • The involvement, influence and power of stakeholder groups, such as pressure groups and the government.
  • Corporate culture and the firm's attitude to CSR and ethics.
  • Compliance costs: human and financial resources needed to carry out CSR policies.
  • Laws and regulations.
Case studies: CSR
  • McDonald's and Burger King: McDonald's created Ronald McDonald House Charities (non profit, in 64 countries and regions) to "directly improve the health and well being of children". Burger King has used humanely sourced meats and eggs since 2007, giving priority and better deals to suppliers of cage free chickens and free range pigs. It runs the Have It Your Way Foundation (hunger, disease prevention, community education) and the McLamore Foundation (scholarships since 2000).
  • Walmart: fined millions of dollars for breaking air and water pollution laws. The media alleged 8% of customers stopped shopping there. Unions criticised practices that led to labour turnover of about 70% in some stores. It was taken to court over unpaid overtime, no proper rest breaks and discrimination against women in pay and promotion.
  • BP Deepwater Horizon: BP's cutting back on safety checks led to the world's largest accidental marine oil spill, costing billions in lost revenue and fines.

  • CSR: considering ethical and environmental practice and acting morally towards stakeholders.
  • Market share: firm's sales revenue as a percentage of industry sales.
Command terms
  • Define / State: give the meaning, or a brief answer without explanation.
  • Distinguish: make differences clear (vision and mission).
  • Explain: give reasons (why objectives are important).
  • Examine: consider the key elements (barriers to meeting objectives).
  • Discuss: balanced review of arguments with a conclusion (whether acting ethically provides competitive advantage).
  • Justify: give valid reasons for an answer (shareholder value or CSR as more sustainable).

Vision is long term and about what the business wants to become. Mission is about what the business does now and why, and is updated more often.

1.4Stakeholders

Definition

Stakeholders are individuals or organizations with a direct interest (known as a stake) in the activities and performance of a business, such as shareholders, employees, customers and suppliers.

  • "Stake" means to have an interest or be involved in something. Stakeholders are impacted by the activities of the organization.
  • They are split into internal and external stakeholders.
Definition

Internal stakeholders of a business are members of the organization, namely the employees, managers, directors and shareholders (owners) of the business.

External stakeholders are individuals and organizations not part of the business but with a direct interest in its activities and performance. Examples include customers, suppliers and the government.

Common mistake: stakeholders and shareholders

Many students treat stakeholders and shareholders as the same thing. Shareholders are the owners of a limited liability company. They are only one stakeholder group, although an important one.

Some shareholders are internal stakeholders (directors or employees who hold shares). Others are external (they own shares but are not involved in daily operations).

Internal stakeholders

(i) Employees

Definition

Employees are the staff of an organization. They have a stake (an interest and involvement) in the organization they work for.

  • Interests: better pay (and other financial benefits), working conditions (hours, physical environment), job security and career progression.
  • Charles Handy argues employees are an organization's most valuable asset: they make the goods and services and talk directly to customers. So more employers involve workers in decision making. A motivated workforce is more dynamic and loyal. Fred Allen: "Treat employees like partners and they act as partners."
  • Richard Branson puts employees first, customers second, shareholders third. He says that way customers and shareholders both benefit. Demotivated workers give poor quality and poor service, and unhappy staff may take industrial action.
Examples of industrial action
  • About 40,000 BMW workers went on a long strike at its South Africa plant in late 2013. Export sales fell by 75%.
  • A 2019 pilots strike at British Airways cost €215 million ($245m) in lost earnings.

(ii) Managers and directors

Definition

Managers are an internal group of stakeholders responsible for overseeing the daily operations of the business.

Directors are senior executives who have been elected by the company's shareholders to address business activities on behalf of their owners.

  • Senior managers and directors may aim to maximise their own benefits, such as annual bonuses and other financial rewards. So they are likely to aim for profit maximization, which also pleases shareholders and helps safeguard the jobs of top executives.
  • They also look at the long term financial health of the organization, e.g. retaining profits for further investment.

(iii) Shareholders

Definition

Shareholders (or stockholders) are the owners of a limited liability company. Shares in a company can be held by individuals and other organizations.

  • They invest money by buying shares. They are a powerful group because they have voting rights and a say in how the company is run, and are entitled to a share of annual profits.
  • Two main interests: (1) to maximise dividends (the part of profits paid to shareholders) and (2) to achieve capital gain (a rise in the value of their shares).
Case study: Nokia and Microsoft

Nokia led the mobile phone market from 1998 to 2008 with up to 49.4% market share and sold its one billionth phone in 2005. By 2007 its market value was $150 billion (5th most valuable brand). Apple and Samsung smartphones forced it to sell to Microsoft for €5.4bn ($7.3bn) in November 2013. 99.5% of its 3,900 shareholders voted for the deal after the share price fell 93% and market share fell to 3%. Its 32,000 mobile division employees moved to Microsoft Mobile in 2014.

Point it proves: a takeover affects each stakeholder group differently (shareholders, employees, customers).

External stakeholders

The six groups are customers, suppliers, financiers, pressure groups, competitors and the government.

(i) Customers

Definition

Customers are the clients of a business. As a key external stakeholder group, customers seek value for money, such as competitive prices and good quality products.

  • Marshall Field: "Right or wrong, the customer is always right." Sam Walton also believed customers can simply spend their money elsewhere, which threatens survival.
  • Interests: greater choice, better quality products, more competitive prices. Bill Gates said dissatisfied customers are the best source of learning for a business.
  • Businesses use market research (Chapter 26), suggestion schemes and satisfaction surveys (fast food restaurants and hotels) to get feedback. Complaints and suggestions go to the management team. This shows a wish to keep customers happy and loyal.

(ii) Suppliers

Definition

Suppliers are an external stakeholder group that provide a business with stocks of raw materials, component parts and finished goods needed for production. They can also provide commercial services, such as maintenance and technical support.

  • Interests: regular contracts at competitive prices, and customers who pay bills on time.
  • Businesses want a good relationship to get quality stock on time at a fair price. Example: long haul airlines rely on caterers. A dispute could cancel flights.
  • A good relationship can win preferential credit terms (buy now, pay later), which improves cash flow.

(iii) Financiers

Definition

Financiers are the financial institutions and individual investors who provide sources of finance for an organization. They are interested in the organization's ability to generate profits and to repay debts.

  • Examples: commercial banks, microfinance providers, individual investors such as business angels.
  • They want the borrower to make enough profit to repay the debt and pay regular interest. They earn money by charging interest.
  • They want a long term relationship so they can lend again if the business needs more loans to expand or run daily operations.

(iv) Pressure groups

Definition

Pressure groups consist of individuals with a common concern (such as environmental protection) who seek to place demands on organizations to act in a particular way or to influence a change in their behaviour.

  • Examples: groups campaigning against smoking, deforestation, harmful treatment of animals, or for protecting the natural environment.
  • They increasingly influence business decisions, often by influencing government policy (lobbying for a change in law). Friends of the Earth and Greenpeace operate internationally and win public support because the public is more aware of environmental damage.
  • The local community is also a source of pressure. Its interests: jobs, extra income and spending in the area, accountability for environmental impact (e.g. tourist litter, traffic congestion, damage to beaches and rural areas), and sponsorship of local fundraising. Local acceptance is paramount to a business being able to operate in the area.
Definition

The local community refers to the general public and local businesses that have a direct interest in the activities of an organization, namely to create jobs and to conduct business activities in a socially responsible way.

Case study: pressure groups in practice

Anti smoking and anti littering campaigns have won support from the public, media and governments. Cigarette advertising is banned in Australia. Littering carries a heavy fine and imprisonment penalty in Singapore. Animal protection groups such as the World Wildlife Fund use ethical marketing to get positive press coverage. Environmental groups often help with beach clean ups.

(v) Competitors

  • Competitors are the rival businesses of an organization. Example: the rivals of Japan Airlines include Singapore Airlines, Qantas, Cathay Pacific and Air China.
  • Competitors may also hold shares in the business. Examples: Cathay Pacific is partly owned by Air China (28.2%) and Qatar Airways (9.4%). The textbook also says Porsche owns more than 31% of Audi (part of the Volkswagen Group).
  • Their objectives: to remain competitive (respond to rivals' activities), to benchmark performance (compare key performance indicators such as sales turnover, profit and market share), and to be creative and innovative (rivalry gives an incentive to keep up with changes such as new production processes or product launches).

(vi) Government

Definition

Government refers to the ruling authority within a state or country. As an external stakeholder group, the government is interested in businesses complying with the law in the conduct of business activities.

The government has a significant influence on business. Its interests include making sure that:

  • Unfair business practices are avoided.
  • The correct amount of corporate tax is paid from net profits.
  • Health and safety standards are met.
  • Employment legislation is followed.
  • Consumer protection laws are upheld.
  • Governments may have a financial stake. Examples: Air Albania is 51% owned by the Albanian government and 49% by Turkish Airlines. The Japanese government owns 53.42% of Tokyo Metro Company.
  • The government can support businesses with lower interest rates and/or taxes to create jobs and investment, incentives to multinationals to locate in the country (subsidised rent, tax concessions), and more investment in roads and communications.
  • It can also constrain business. The textbook says Microsoft was broken up into two smaller companies after lawsuits for malpractice and unfair competition.

Conflict between stakeholders

Definition

Stakeholder conflict refers to differences in the varying needs and priorities of the various stakeholder groups of a business.

Conflict refers to situations where stakeholders have disputes or differences regarding certain issues. This can lead to arguments and tension between the stakeholder groups.

  • A business cannot meet all stakeholder objectives at the same time. Conflict comes from disagreements in opinions or values. Margaret Thatcher's point: standing in the middle of a road gets you hit by traffic from both sides, so you cannot please everyone all the time.

Causes of conflict, with examples

  • Director remuneration (pay and benefits). Shareholders and employees may say top managers are "overpaid" and that profits should be shared more fairly (dividends, better pay for staff). Senior executives say their pay must reflect the higher risks of decision making, and this raises profits and so dividends and wages later. In 2021 the Economic Policy Institute found CEOs of the top 350 US companies were paid 351 times more than a typical worker.
  • Shareholders versus employees: if shareholders want a bigger share of profit, staff benefits such as profit related or performance related pay may be cut, which upsets employees.
  • Suppliers versus the business: suppliers want customers to pay full price in one go to help their cash flow. Businesses expect discounts for regular and bulk purchases.
  • Stakeholders with more than one role: managers are employees too. Some employees are also shareholders. A customer may also be in the local community. So their interests can conflict.

How managers decide who to prioritise

  • The type of business entity: a partnership may aim for profit maximization and see customers as key. A charity may see the local community as most important. A limited liability company is accountable to its shareholders, so they come first.
  • The goals and objectives of the business: if the aim is growth and expansion, the share of profits to owners matters less in the short term, and managers and directors may be given higher priority to help the change process.
  • The source and degree of power (influence) of each group: customers have more power in a mass market with many substitutes. Access to the media gives pressure groups more power. A united workforce through a trade union (Chapter 13) strengthens employees.

Modern management thinking says there are mutual benefits in meeting the competing needs of stakeholders at the same time. For example, meeting the needs of employees and managers builds a cohesive, motivated, loyal workforce with low absenteeism and labour turnover. This leads to better customer relations, better image, higher market share and higher long term profit, which pleases shareholders. More output may also create jobs in the local community. So meeting all needs is possible, but maybe only in the medium to long term.

Case studies: stakeholder conflict
  • Skoda Auto (founded 1895, part of Volkswagen Group since 2000): entered China in 2007 for growth, but its European workforce struck over pay. The strike cost 60 million crowns ($2.9m) a day in lost output. China later became its main market (one in four Skoda cars sold there).
  • Royal Bank of Scotland Group: before the 2008 crisis it was one of the world's largest banks. It could not cope with the turmoil, so the UK government bought over 80% of its shares. The share price fell from £3.54 ($4.95) to £0.11 ($0.16). It sold its 4.26% stake in Bank of China without profit and made the largest loss in British corporate history, £24.1bn ($33.8bn) in 2008.
  • Royal Dutch Shell: formed in 1907 by the merger of Royal Dutch (Netherlands) and Shell (UK). In 2020 net income was $21.5 billion (about $2.45m per hour) and sales revenue above $180.5 billion. Internal stakeholders watch profit closely, while pressure groups such as Greenpeace scrutinise its activities.

Stakeholders and the key concepts: managing conflict

  • Conflict exists in every organization and needs careful management. Employees disagree with change, customers are unhappy if the business acts unethically, suppliers fail to deliver on time, shareholders dislike poor performance. So conflict resolution is an important part of a sustainable business strategy.
  • It is unlikely a business can meet every stakeholder's goals all the time, but it is unrealistic or undesirable to maximise the needs of just one group. A group that is ignored may cause interruptions and problems. Most strategies aim for a "best fit" compromise.
  • The outcome of negotiation depends on organizational culture (Chapter 11) and the relative bargaining power of each stakeholder. A large multinational like Honda or Ford has more bargaining power with suppliers than a sole trader mechanic buying parts.
  • Pressure groups are more effective because of social media. Public awareness of globalisation's harms (child labour, global warming, sustainability) makes it harder to ignore ethics.
  • How managers deal with conflict depends on their leadership style (Chapter 9) and organizational culture. Paternalistic leaders would say employees' needs come first: hire the right people, train them, and they will give customers first rate service.
  • Jack Ma (Alibaba, before its $25 billion IPO) wrote "Customers first, employees second and shareholders third", because "If the customer is happy, the business is happy and the shareholders are happy."

1.5Growth and evolution

Economies and diseconomies of scale

Definition

Economies of scale refer to lower average costs of production as a firm operates on a larger scale due to gains in productive efficiency, such as easier and cheaper access to sources of finance.

Average cost refers to the cost per unit of output.

A major reason businesses grow is to gain economies of scale. Lower average costs can give a competitive cost advantage: lower prices for customers, a higher profit margin on each item, or both.

Worked example: average cost

Average cost (AC) = total costs (TC) ÷ quantity of output (Q)

If total costs of making 10,000 shirts are $78,000, then AC = $78,000 ÷ 10,000 = $7.80 per shirt.

AC has two parts: average fixed cost AFC = total fixed cost (TFC) ÷ Q, and average variable cost AVC = total variable cost (TVC) ÷ Q. So AC = AFC + AVC.

Why AFC falls: total fixed cost stays the same but is spread over more and more output, so AFC falls all the time as output rises.

Definition

The optimal level of output is the most efficient scale of operation for a business. This occurs at the level of output where the average cost of production is minimized.

Diseconomies of scale are the cost disadvantages of growth. Average costs are likely to eventually rise as a firm grows due to a lack of control, coordination and communication.

Firms minimize costs by operating at the optimal level of output. Economies inside the firm and within its control are internal. Those within the industry and generally beyond one firm's control are external.

Internal economies of scale

Definition

Internal economies of scale occur within a particular organization (rather than the industry as a whole) as it grows in size.

A business can reduce average cost through any mix of these types. Which matters most depends on the business.

Case studies
  • Airbus A380: the world's largest passenger plane has 49% more seating than the Boeing 787 Dreamliner but burns 17% less fuel per seat, so fuel cost per seat is lower. Common mistake

Many students define economies of scale as "the benefits of bulk buying". Even small businesses can buy in bulk. The term means the cost saving benefits of large scale operations. Financial, technical and managerial economies are probably far more important than bulk buying in cutting average costs for most businesses.

External economies of scale

Definition

External economies of scale occur when an organization's average cost falls as the industry grows. Hence all firms in the industry benefit. They are cost saving benefits that arise from outside the business, due to its favourable location or the general growth of the industry.

ExampleExplanation

Internal diseconomies of scale

Definition

Internal diseconomies of scale occur due to internal problems of mismanagement, causing average costs of production to increase as a firm grows.

Businesses can become too large. There is a "tipping point" when economies of scale stop. The business becomes outsized and inefficient and average cost begins to rise (see Figure 5.1).

  • Lack of control and coordination: the span of control (Chapter 8) increases, which causes communication problems and slows decisions. Coordination is harder with operations in different countries. Workers in large organizations may feel alienated, harming morale (Chapter 10). Costs rise with no extra productivity.
  • Poorer working relationships: senior managers become detached from lower levels and feel out of touch. This damages communication and motivation, lowers productivity and raises average costs.
  • Disadvantages of specialization and division of labour: workers get bored with repetitive tasks. With a bigger workforce there may be slack (inefficiency and procrastination). Productive efficiency falls and average costs rise.
  • Bureaucracy (excessive administration, paperwork and company policies): decisions take longer, costs rise without a proportional rise in output, and communication gets worse.
  • Complacency (lack of awareness of genuine risks or deficiencies) in a large, dominant firm or market leader reduces productivity and raises unit costs.

Because large firms may suffer diseconomies, some businesses grow by franchising instead. McDonald's and 7-Eleven expanded and raised brand awareness without the higher average costs of being large.

External diseconomies of scale

Definition

External diseconomies of scale occur due to factors beyond the firm's control which cause average costs of production to increase as an industry grows. They affect the whole industry, often because too many firms compete in the market.

  • Higher rents: too many businesses in one area make land scarcer, so rents rise. Fixed costs rise for all firms with no extra output. Examples: Manhattan (New York), Shinjuku (Tokyo), The City (London), Silicon Valley.
  • Higher wages: workers have more choice of employers, so firms must pay more to keep or attract staff, raising average costs without raising output.
  • Traffic congestion: too many businesses in one area delay deliveries and raise transport costs and unit costs.

Internal and external growth

Definition

Internal growth (also known as organic growth) occurs when a business grows using its own capabilities and resources to increase the scale of its operations and sales revenue.

External growth (or inorganic growth) occurs when a business grows and evolves by collaborating with, buying up or merging with other organizations.

Internal growth is usually financed through retained profits, borrowing and issuing new shares.

Methods of internal growth

  • Changing price: more customers buy at lower prices. If there are few substitutes, demand is price inelastic (Chapter 28), so raising price earns more revenue. In highly competitive markets demand is price elastic, so a price cut gives proportionately more sales revenue.
  • Improved promotion: people buy if they are informed, reminded or persuaded. Coca-Cola spends about $3bn a year on promotion and is the world's most recognised brand, with sales of 1.9 billion servings a day.
  • Producing improved or better products: market research, innovation and new product development. Most new products fail, so firms often improve an existing product. Apple grows by launching new products (iPod, iPad, iPhone, iMac Pro, MacBook Air and Pro, Apple TV, Apple Watch, HomePod, AirPod, iTunes, App Store).
  • Selling through a greater distribution network (placement): wide availability helps sales. The Lamborghini Veneno ($4.5m, very limited supply) has few potential customers. Coca-Cola is sold in supermarkets, wholesalers, restaurants, cinemas, aeroplanes and vending machines. More outlets cost more but help growth.
  • Increased capital expenditure (investment spending): new locations, new production processes and technologies to raise productivity. Risks may not pay off, so careful planning and investment appraisal (Chapter 21) may be needed.
  • Improved training and development: employees are a firm's most important asset. Customers will not buy from salespeople who lack product knowledge. Training builds confidence, motivation, better customer service, loyalty and sales.
  • Offer preferential credit: "buy now, pay later" or instalments over 12 or 24 months for cars, furniture, large TVs. Too much credit harms cash flow (Chapter 20).
  • Providing overall value for money: customers look beyond price to quality, after sales care, brand image, maintenance costs and environmental considerations.
Case study: Poundland (internal growth)

Founded 1990. Its 500th store opened in 2014 and it had over 800 stores at the start of 2022. It sells most products for £1 ($1.40) or less, serves 7 million customers a week, and has reported profit rises of up to 29%. Its growth strategy is expansion in Ireland, the Netherlands and the UK to gain market share.

All businesses have an appropriate scale of operation. The market for smartphones is huge, but the market for IB textbooks is smaller. Size is measured in relative terms by comparing with competitors. Ways to measure size:

A rise in any of these suggests the firm is growing.

External growth methods

(i) Mergers and acquisitions (M&As)

Definition

A merger is a form of external growth whereby two (or more) firms agree to form a new organization, thereby losing their original identities.

An acquisition is a method of external growth that involves one company buying a controlling interest (majority stake) in another company, with the agreement and approval of the target company's Board of Directors.

Synergy is a benefit of growth, which occurs when the whole is greater than the sum of the individual parts when two or more business operations are combined. Synergy creates greater output and improved efficiency.

The target company is the organization that is purchased by another in an acquisition or takeover. The purchaser is the acquiring company in an acquisition or the buyer of another company in a takeover.

  • "M&As" means the consolidation or integration of two or more businesses into a single company. The new, larger entity usually benefits from synergies such as economies of scale and has a larger market share. M&As also need restructuring of the organizational structure (Chapter 8).
  • Merger: two or more firms agree to create a new company with its own legal identity. It usually needs a new name and a Board of Directors made up of executives from the firms involved. Examples: BP and Amoco (1998, now just "BP"), Daimler Benz and Chrysler (1998), Glaxo Wellcome and SmithKline Beecham (2000), Hewlett-Packard and Compaq (2001), American Airlines and US Airways (2013), Just Eat and Takeaway.com (2020).
  • Acquisition: the acquiring company buys enough shares to hold a majority stake. Usually the larger, financially stronger company buys the smaller one, so the two are not of equal stature (a merger usually joins firms of similar size). Examples: Google bought YouTube for $1.65bn in 2006. Amazon bought Whole Foods for $13.7bn in 2017. Whole Foods keeps its name but Amazon, the parent company, controls its operations.
Common mistake

Students often say a business must buy at least 51% of shares to acquire a company. The textbook says buying 50% plus 1 share guarantees control, but this may be too expensive and is not a necessary condition. Buying a majority stake (which can be less than 50%) can still give control. (Note: earlier the textbook says the purchaser "must buy more than 50%" for absolute control.)

(ii) Takeovers

Definition

A takeover (also called a hostile takeover) occurs when a company buys a controlling interest in another firm without the prior agreement or approval of the target company's Board of Directors.

  • To persuade the target's shareholders to sell, the offer price is likely to be well above the stock market value of the shares.
  • Examples: Heineken took over many Dutch competitors to dominate Dutch brewing. Kraft Foods took over Cadbury in 2010 for $18 billion. Walt Disney bought 21st Century Fox in 2017 for $52.4 billion. Disney bought Pixar for $7.4 billion in 2006 (high demand for merchandise earned Disney over $3.2 billion; Toy Story 3 in 2010 earned over $1bn at the box office) and Lucasfilm in 2012 (Star Wars third trilogy).
Box 5.1: Reasons why businesses become takeover targets
  • They have growth potential but lack enough funds for internal growth.
  • They are seen as a small rival with growth potential.
  • They have a widely recognised corporate name or brand but face a liquidity or financial crisis.
  • They are vulnerable (an easy target) because of a drop in profits and a fall in their share price.

(iii) Joint ventures

Definition

A joint venture (JV) is a growth strategy that combines the contributions and responsibilities of two or more different organizations in a shared project by creating a separate legal enterprise.

  • Two or more businesses split the costs, risks, control and rewards of a project. Typically a 50:50 split of costs, responsibilities and profits (or losses).
  • Examples: Coca-Cola and San Miguel share a bottling plant in the Philippines. BMW and Brilliance Auto have had a JV since 2003 to produce, distribute and sell BMW cars in China.
  • JVs give benefits similar to M&As without losing individual corporate identities.
Advantages of joint venturesDrawbacks
Synergy: pooled skills, experience and resources. Bank of Scotland and Sainsbury's (50:50, 1997 to 2014) each specialised in what they did best while gaining new technology and customers.Partners rely heavily on each other's resources and goodwill.
Exploitation of local knowledge: use partners' local knowledge and reputation, avoiding overseas problems (etiquette, cultural values, languages, traditions) of M&As.There may be dilution of brands, though firms spend huge sums building their own brands (Chapter 27).
Spreading of costs and risks: costs, risks and losses shared. Products can be diversified.Organizational culture clashes can cause operational problems and conflict.
Entry to foreign markets: agreements with local firms. Sometimes national law makes JVs the only way in. Many foreign firms entered China and India through JVs.
Relatively cheap: M&As have lengthy legal and admin costs, and the cost of a hostile takeover is often unknown. JVs are cheaper to set up and easier to leave.
Competitive advantages: less competition between the partners, and a stronger force against rivals. Larger combined size may bring more economies of scale.
High success rate: JVs tend to be friendly and well received by stakeholders. Takeovers often fail because they are hostile.
Case study: Tiffany & Co. and Swatch Group

In 2007 Swatch and Tiffany formed a JV to make watches under the brand Tiffany Watch Co, for 20 years. Disputes began in 2011 and Swatch cancelled it, accusing Tiffany of blocking development. In 2014 Tiffany was ordered to pay Swatch almost $450m plus $9m in legal fees.

Point it proves: JVs can fail through conflict and culture clashes.

(iv) Strategic alliances

Definition

Strategic alliances are formed when two or more organizations join together to benefit from external growth, without having to set up a new separate legal entity.

  • Like a JV: two or more businesses cooperate for mutual benefit and share the costs of product development, operations and marketing. Unlike a JV: the firms stay independent and do not form a new legal entity.
  • Main purpose: synergies from each member's strengths by pooling resources (expertise and finance). Members also get economies of scale. Customers may get value added services such as wider access to distribution (Chapter 30).
  • Airline example: two airlines with half empty planes could compete, or cooperate by using one full plane to cut crew and fuel costs and split the profit.
StageWhat happens

2. Partnership assessmentAnalyse potential partners: human and financial resources, skills, experience, expertise.

Joint ventures and strategic alliances have similar drawbacks (reliance on partners, brand dilution, culture clashes).

Case study: Star Alliance

Set up in 1997 by Lufthansa, Scandinavian Airlines, Thai Airways International, United Airlines and Air Canada (headquarters in Frankfurt). The first strategic airline alliance, now with 26 members and a pre pandemic passenger count of 762.27 million. It also has 40 non voting affiliated carriers, so it serves almost every country.

(v) Franchising

Definition

Franchising refers to an agreement between a franchisor selling its rights to other businesses (franchisees) to allow them to sell products under its corporate name in return for a fee and regular royalty payments.

  • A franchise is a form of business ownership where a person or business buys a licence to trade using another company's products, name, logos, brands and trademarks.
  • The buyer (franchisee) pays a licence fee to the parent company (franchisor) and also pays a royalty (like commission) based on sales revenue.
  • Franchisors include McDonald's, Subway, The Body Shop, 7-Eleven, Baskin-Robbins, Burger King, Hertz, Intercontinental Hotels, KFC, Marriott, Pizza Hut.

  • Pizza Hut: founded 1958, the world's largest pizza chain. First franchise within a year. Over 18,700 restaurants in over 95 countries.

Growth, evolution and the key concepts

  • Growth is a strategic objective for many businesses. The driving force is not only profit but sustainable competitiveness. Chinese proverb: "Be not afraid of going slowly; be afraid only of standing still."
  • M&As are common in today's fast paced, competitive and changing environment. Organizations have to "run faster to stay still": change and be creative to grow and evolve sustainably, to keep market share and competitiveness.
  • A strategy suitable for one organization may not suit another, so put answers in the context of the business and consider short and long term factors.
  • Growth does not always work. Businesses may choose to shrink: break away from a strategic alliance, end a joint venture, or demerge.
Definition

A demerger occurs when a company sells off a part of its business, thereby separating into two or more businesses. It usually happens due to conflicts, inefficiencies and incompatibilities after an earlier merger of two or more companies.

Reasons for shrinking:

  • Offloading unprofitable sections of the business.
  • Avoiding rising average costs and inefficiencies from being too large (internal and external diseconomies of scale).
  • Raising cash to keep other, more profitable parts running.
  • A clearer corporate focus on a smaller range of products or operations.

Growth and downsizing can happen in cycles. Downsizing creates problems: redundancies must be planned and managed sensitively and ethically. For large firms the cost of redundancies can be high and cause short term liquidity problems (Chapter 20).

1.6Multinational companies (MNCs)

What is a multinational company?

Definition

A multinational company (MNC) is an organization that operates in two or more countries, with its head office usually based in the home country.

A host country is any nation that allows a multinational company to set up in its country.

  • The terms MNC and transnational corporation are often used for the same thing. Some commentators say an MNC has its head office in the home country, whereas a transnational corporation has regional head offices rather than a single international base.
  • Examples of large MNCs: Apple, Coca-Cola, Louis Vuitton, Nike, Samsung, Toyota. They stand out in their industries for sales revenue, profits, capital assets and market value.
  • The rise of MNCs and the growing importance of international trade have intensified globalization.

Why businesses become MNCs

ReasonExplanation
Cheaper production costsEspecially inexpensive labour. Labour is costly in Germany, partly due to minimum wage laws, so Adidas, BMW and Volkswagen have production facilities overseas where labour is more affordable.
Economies of scaleAs production rises, MNCs gain economies of scale (see 1.5).
Host country benefitsGood infrastructure (roads, telecommunications, ports). Better quantity, quality or cost of land. Financial incentives from the host government that cut average production cost and help the MNC enter new markets.
Increased customer baseA larger customer base means higher sales revenue. KFC, Starbucks, Lamborghini and Walmart expanded into China for its huge customer base.
Brand development and brand valueOperating internationally can raise brand value. Global marketing strategies can raise brand recognition and brand loyalty (Chapter 27).
Avoiding protectionist policiesBy producing inside a country, MNCs usually avoid the trade barriers it imposes. This is why Toyota, Honda and Nissan built factories in the European Union and North America.
Spreading risksBad conditions in one country or region may not damage the whole business. Natural disasters (tsunamis), terrorism (9-11 attacks) and disease (swine flu, mad cow disease) have hit different parts of the world. Over specialization in one region could have cut profit seriously.
Definition

Protectionist policies are measures imposed by a country to reduce the competitiveness of imports, such as tariffs (import taxes), quotas and restrictive trade practices.

The impact of MNCs on host countries

MNCs affect host countries in ways that can be good or bad.

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the value of a country's annual output or national income.

Case study: Hollywood

Hollywood is a threat to many movie industries around the world, so governments often step in to protect their domestic industries and jobs. China and India limit how many Hollywood movies are released each month. China has an official annual quota of 34 foreign movies, while India is the largest producer of movies in the world.

Point it proves: host governments may use protectionist policies against MNC competition.

Multinational companies and the key concepts

  • Change, ethics, creativity and sustainability are central to any strategy that involves growth in overseas markets.
  • International trade causes problems such as adjusting to foreign customs and cultures. So a business may need to change its practices in a creative and ethical way to suit local laws and regulations. Understanding cultural dimensions (Hofstede, Chapter 54) helps firms operating overseas.
  • Change is not always sustainable. International M&As or strategic alliances do not always work, because of resistance to change and cultural clashes. Then a demerger may happen (sale of part of a business, separating into two or more entities, see 1.5). Examples: Cadbury Schweppes (merged 1969) split in 2008. DaimlerChrysler (merged 1998) demerged in 2007.
  • Brand acquisition: an alternative to a full merger or takeover is to buy one of the target company's brands. BMW bought the Rolls Royce brand in 2003 to improve and sustain its broad product portfolio (Chapters 27 and 47). It is relatively low risk but can be expensive. In 2013 Berkshire Hathaway bought the Heinz brand for $28 billion. A firm may also sell off a brand to deal with a liquidity problem (Chapter 20) rather than be bought by another firm, which would cause more disruptive change.
