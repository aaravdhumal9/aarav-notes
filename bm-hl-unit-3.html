3.1Introduction to finance

Syllabus: the role of finance for businesses, capital expenditure and revenue expenditure (AO2).

Why businesses need finance

The role or purpose of finance for a business can be split into two groups: capital expenditure and revenue expenditure. Every spending decision a business makes falls into one of these two groups.

Capital expenditure

Definition

Capital expenditure refers to investment spending on fixed assets, such as the purchase of machinery, equipment, land and buildings.

Definition

Fixed assets (or noncurrent assets) are items of monetary value that have a long term function for businesses, so can be used repeatedly for the purpose of production.

Definition

Collateral refers to the financial guarantee for securing external loan capital to finance investment expenditure for business growth.

  • Fixed assets are not intended to be sold in the short term. They are used to produce goods and services.
  • Examples: land, buildings, equipment, machinery and commercial vehicles.
  • Fixed assets give long term benefits to the business. They also decide the scale (size) of the business's operations.
  • The money for capital expenditure usually comes from long term sources of finance (see 3.2), because fixed assets cost a lot.
  • Fixed assets can act as collateral. This helps the business to get extra loans to pay for growth.

Reasons for capital expenditure

  • To add extra production capacity as the business grows.
  • To improve efficiency by using the latest technology, including IT systems and production technology.
  • To replace worn out, damaged or obsolete (outdated) capital equipment and machinery.
  • To comply with changing laws and regulations, for example green technologies.

Challenges of capital expenditure

  • The high cost of fixed assets.
  • The limited sources of finance available for such investments. Because of this, some investments are simply not feasible.
  • Managers often have to look at the expected return on investment using investment appraisal methods (covered in a later chapter of the textbook) before they decide.

Revenue expenditure

Definition

Revenue expenditure refers to spending on the day to day running of a business, such as payment of rent, wages, salaries and utility bills.

  • Other examples: raw materials. It also includes indirect costs such as insurance and advertising.
  • Revenue expenditure creates value for the business today, not in the future.
  • Expenses come from producing goods and services. This production generates revenue.
  • Costs must be controlled so that sales revenue is enough to cover production costs. Only then can the business earn a profit.

Who uses which sources of finance

Different businesses can reach different sources of finance. The main source for sole traders is their personal savings. Larger and more established businesses can use other sources for capital expenditure, such as selling shares on a stock exchange. All of these sources are covered in 3.2.

Finance and the key concepts

  • Change: capital expenditure helps a business to change. More people working from home meant businesses had to buy laptops, smartphones and other hardware, and pay for cloud based software subscriptions.
  • Staying competitive: businesses must invest in their future. Some businesses collapse because they do not spend enough on capital expenditure to adapt to changes in the external environment, such as changing consumer trends or competitors moving in a new strategic direction.
  • Revenue expenditure matters too: attractive and competitive pay packages motivate the workforce. This improves productivity and employee loyalty.
  • Sustainability: both types of expenditure are vital for the long term survival of a business.
Real world example

The London Olympic Games. London was the first city to host the modern Olympics three times (1908, 1948 and 2012). Around half a million new jobs were added in the UK construction industry to prepare for the 2012 Games. Transport for London paid for many improvements, including bigger railway networks and Britain's first high speed ("bullet") trains. Point it shows: large capital expenditure on infrastructure with long lasting benefits, and benefits for many stakeholders such as construction workers and travellers.

Worked example

A cafe pays $4,000 for a new coffee machine, $900 for this month's rent and $350 for coffee beans. Classify each payment.

Step 1: ask "will the item be used repeatedly over a long time?"
Coffee machine: yes. It is a fixed asset, so it is capital expenditure ($4,000).
Step 2: ask "is it a day to day running cost?"
Rent: yes, revenue expenditure ($900). Coffee beans: yes, raw materials, revenue expenditure ($350).
Total revenue expenditure this month is $900 + $350 = $1,250.

3.2Sources of finance

Syllabus: internal sources (personal funds for sole traders, retained profit, sale of assets) and external sources (share capital, loan capital, overdrafts, trade credit, crowdfunding, leasing, microfinance providers, business angels), both AO2. Appropriateness of short or long term sources of finance for a given situation (AO3).

What are sources of finance?

Definition

Sources of finance is the general term used to refer to where or how businesses obtain their funds, such as from personal funds, retained profits, loan capital and share capital.

All businesses need money for the set up of the business, for day to day running (revenue expenditure) and for expansion (capital expenditure). Sources of finance are split into internal and external.

Definition

Internal sources of finance are funds generated from within the organization, namely through personal funds, retained profits and the sale of assets.

Definition

External sources of finance are the funds from outside of the organization, such as through debt (overdrafts and loan capital), share capital and business angels.

Internal sources of finance

(i) Personal funds

Definition

Personal funds are a source of internal finance, referring to the use of an entrepreneur's own savings. Personal funds are usually used to finance business start ups for sole traders.

  • Also called owner's capital. It is the main source of finance for sole traders.
  • For most sole traders it is not enough, so they need other sources too.
  • Example: chef Jamie Oliver used £500,000 ($720,000) of his own money to fund his restaurant "Fifteen", which hired fifteen unknown recruits.

(ii) Retained profit

Definition

Retained profit is the value of the surplus that a business keeps to use within the business after paying corporate taxes on its profits to the government and dividend payments to its shareholders.

  • Often used for capital expenditure: buying or upgrading fixed assets.
  • Some may be kept in a contingency fund for emergencies, crises and unforeseen spending.
  • Benefit: no interest charges.

(iii) Sale of assets

Definition

Sale of assets means selling existing items of value that the business owns, such as dormant assets (unused assets) and obsolete assets (outdated assets).

  • Examples: selling obsolete machinery and old computers that have just been replaced.
  • If a business relocates, it may sell land and buildings.
  • In extreme cases, a business can sell subsidiaries to deal with a major liquidity threat to its survival.

External sources of finance

(i) Share capital

Definition

Share capital is the money raised from selling shares in a limited liability company.

Definition

An initial public offering (IPO) refers to a business converting its legal status to a publicly traded company by floating (or selling) its shares on a stock exchange for the first time.

Definition

A share issue (or share placement) means an existing publicly held company raises further finance by selling more of its shares.

Definition

A stock exchange is a highly regulated marketplace where individuals and businesses can buy and/or sell shares in publicly traded companies.

  • Share capital is the main source of finance for most limited liability companies. It is recorded on the company's balance sheet.
  • Key advantage: it can provide a huge amount of finance. Petrobras (Brazil) raised a world record $70 billion from a public share issue in September 2010.
  • Privately held companies cannot sell shares to the general public. Publicly held companies can sell shares on a stock exchange.
  • A stock exchange lets companies raise capital and provides a market for second hand shares and government stocks. Large ones include the London, Tokyo and New York Stock Exchanges.
  • Many businesses "go public" by floating shares for the first time (an IPO). Popular IPOs are heavily oversubscribed, which pushes up the share price.
  • Disadvantages: ownership and control become diluted. The process also involves many legal and administrative procedures with costs.
Real world example

Agricultural Bank of China (ABC). In August 2010 ABC completed its IPO in Shanghai and Hong Kong and raised $22.1 billion. This was the largest flotation in the world at the time, beating the $21.9 billion raised by the IPO of ICBC (Industrial and Commercial Bank of China) in October 2006. Investors still put in money despite weak sentiment in Asian stock markets and fears that shares were overvalued. Today ABC has over 320 million customers in 24,000 branches. Point it shows: an IPO can raise a very large sum, and large well known firms attract investors.

Common mistake

Students often write that a fall in a company's share price reduces its profits. It is more likely the other way round. Poor performance lowers the value of the company, and then the share price falls.

Exam tip

When shareholders sell their shares, the company does not receive that money. These shares are traded on the secondary market of the stock exchange, and no new shares have been issued by the company.

(ii) Loan capital

Definition

Loan capital (or debt capital) refers to medium to long term sources of interest bearing finance obtained from commercial lenders. Examples include mortgages, business development loans and debentures.

  • Interest is charged and can be fixed or variable, depending on the agreement.
  • The money is paid back in instalments over a set time, such as 5, 10 or 25 years.
TypeWhat it is
MortgageA secured loan to buy property (land or buildings). If the borrower defaults (fails to repay), the lender can repossess the property.
Business development loanA highly flexible loan made to fit the borrower's needs. It can be used to start or expand a business, buy specialist equipment and assets, or improve cash flow.
DebentureA long term loan issued by a business. Holders (individuals, governments or other businesses) get interest even if the business makes a loss, and before shareholders get any dividends. Interest can be fixed or variable.
  • Debenture holders have no ownership or voting rights, so the business keeps control. Debentures give long term finance without the business losing control.
  • Drawback: issuing debentures raises the firm's gearing ratio (more borrowing as a percentage of total capital employed). The firm is then more at risk if interest rates rise.
Real world examples

Kellett School, Hong Kong (2013). The school sold record breaking debentures worth up to HK$10m, refundable when students leave, to help pay for a new HK$200m campus (auditorium, swimming pool, library and more). Fees ranged from HK$123,500 to HK$168,800 a year. Critics said the high cost of education could put off skilled expat families. A rival school, Harrow International, offers debentures of up to HK$3m that carry no interest. Debentures are common in international schools in Hong Kong because of the costs of capital expenditure and land.

Arsenal Football Club used debentures to help build the Emirates Stadium in London.

(iii) Overdrafts

Definition

Overdrafts allow a business to spend in excess of the amount in its bank account, up to a pre determined limit. They are the most flexible form of borrowing for most businesses in the short term.

  • Commonly used for minor cash flow problems.
  • Interest can be relatively high, but overdrafts are usually cheaper than bank loans. This is because they are short term and interest is charged daily and only if the business is overdrawn.
  • Suitable when: a large cash outflow is needed (for example a retailer stocking up for the peak season), or when the business has sold on trade credit and is waiting to be paid.
  • Disadvantage: overdrafts are repayable on demand by the lender.

(iv) Trade credit

Definition

Trade credit allows a business to postpone payments or to "buy now and pay later". The credit provider does not receive any cash from the buyer until a later date (usually between 30 to 60 days).

Definition

Creditors are suppliers who allow a business to purchase goods and/or services on trade credit. The customers who owe the money are called debtors.

  • Postponing payments to suppliers helps to ease cash flow problems.
  • Credit cards are similar, but the creditor is a financial institution such as a commercial bank (for example Visa or American Express). It acts as a cash advance to the card holder. Credit cards are a vital source for sole traders and partnerships.

(v) Crowdfunding

Definition

Crowdfunding is the practice of raising finance for a business venture or project by getting small amounts of money from a large number of people, usually through online platforms.

  • It is the opposite of the traditional method of borrowing a large sum from a small number of lenders.
  • It uses online and social media platforms (which charge a hosting fee) and also networks of friends, family and work colleagues.
  • Donation crowdfunding: people give money that the business does not have to pay back.
  • The more creative or innovative the idea, the more likely it is to raise funds this way.
  • In many countries crowdfunding is heavily regulated to protect donors and prevent fraud.
  • Drawback: start ups that pitch for investors often lose some ownership and control, because investors ask for shares in the new company.

(vi) Leasing

Definition

Leasing is a form of hiring whereby a lessee pays rental income to hire assets from the lessor, the legal owner of the assets.

  • It can be cheaper to lease assets such as machinery, equipment, computers, vehicles and buildings, especially in the short to medium term.
  • Suitable for businesses that do not have the initial capital to buy. This releases cash for other uses.
  • The lessor is responsible for repairs and maintenance.
  • Spending on leased assets counts as a business expense, which can reduce the tax bill of the lessee.
  • Disadvantage: in the long term, leasing is more expensive than hire purchase or buying the asset outright.
Related methodHow it works
Sale and leasebackA business sells a fixed asset to raise finance and immediately leases it back. Ownership is transferred but the asset does not physically leave the business.
Hire purchase (HP)The business pays for the asset in instalments (for example over 12 or 24 months). The asset is legally owned by the creditor until the last payment. A deposit is often needed. If the buyer defaults, the lender can repossess the asset. Interest is charged. Difference from leasing: the buyer eventually owns the asset.
Real world example

Wizz Air. In late 2013 the Hungarian low cost airline signed a sale and leaseback deal with COB Leasing Co. of China to finance six Airbus A320 aircraft. The deal raised $549 million for expansion to new routes (Barcelona, Bologna, Dortmund and Rome from 2014). In 2020, because of COVID 19, Wizz Air carried 16.6 million passengers, only 42% of the 40 million in the year before. Established in 2003, it flies to 150 destinations in 44 countries. Point it shows: a sale and leaseback frees up large sums tied up in assets. Police cars are another common leased asset.

(vii) Microfinance providers

Definition

Microfinance is a type of financial service aimed at entrepreneurs of small businesses, especially females and those on low incomes.

  • Microfinance providers are social enterprises. They help disadvantaged people to get essential financial services and help reduce poverty.
  • Without them, small businesses have very limited access to banking and insurance. Often the only finance comes from unofficial money lenders at extremely high cost.
  • The funds are approved for microenterprise development.
  • According to the World Bank, over 500 million people have benefited directly, yet nearly 3 billion people in low income countries have little or no access to official financial services.
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Accessibility: helps people in poverty become financially independent. Commercial banks do not usually lend such small amounts to this group.Immorality: critics say it is unethical because the providers are for profit, so they profit from the poor and unemployed.
Job creation: good use of microfinance creates new jobs, which helps society.Limited finance: only small sums are offered because of the high risk of default (failure to repay).
Social wellbeing: successful applicants are less likely to pull their children out of school and get better access to healthcare for their families.Limited eligibility: not all poor people qualify. Providers are for profit, so they must make sure borrowers can repay.
Real world example

BRAC (formerly the Bangladesh Rural Advancement Committee) is the largest microfinance provider in Bangladesh. Founded in 1972 with its head office in Dhaka, it has over 97,700 staff, most of them women, and has helped more than 126 million people in 11 countries.

(viii) Business angels

Definition

Business angels are extremely wealthy individuals who risk their own money by investing in small to medium sized businesses that have high growth potential.

  • They back high risk, high return ventures, often firms that cannot get enough finance from commercial banks and are too small to attract shareholders.
  • They often take a proactive role in setting up or running the business, so the owner loses some control.
  • The owner may later need to buy out the angel's stake to regain control and ownership.
  • Advantage: their experience and financial backing can be a major help to the survival and success of a new business.

What business angels look for

CriterionMeaning
Return on investmentThey want a positive return. Most start ups fail, so each investment needs good potential to be highly profitable.
The business planSets out the purpose and goals of the venture. They want to see that the firm understands its market, can compete in a high growth market with few competitors, and has creative and original ideas.
PeopleNo one person has all the skills, experience and contacts. Success needs a good team. Poor people management is a major cause of business failure.

Real world examples

UK business angels. According to the UK Business Angels Association, about 18,000 angels invest around £1.5 billion a year in the UK. A typical angel invests about £40,000, for up to eight years, and is driven by helping others succeed. Only about 5% of Britain's angel investors are female.

Tawfeer Market (Egypt). An online grocery business founded in 2020 that delivers in Alexandria. In early 2022 it raised $500,000 from business angels. Egypt's grocery market is worth over $50 billion, so more competitors are expected.

Choosing between short term and long term sources (AO3)

Definition

Short term sources of finance are those available for a period of less than one year, used to pay for the daily or routine operations of the business, such as overdrafts and trade credit.

Definition

Long term sources of finance are those available for any period of more than 12 months from the accounting period, used for the purchase of fixed assets or to finance the expansion of a business.

  • For external finance, short term means anything that has to be repaid within the current fiscal (tax) year or the next twelve months. Examples: trade credit, overdrafts, crowdfunding.
  • Long term examples: mortgages, debentures, share capital, leasing, microfinance and business angels.
  • There is no common definition of short and long term. They vary between businesses, industries and countries. What matters is how they link to the business's objectives and purpose.
  • Example: businesses with heavy research and development (space technology, pharmaceuticals) may see the long term as 10 or more years. Fast paced industries such as ICT and fashion may see 5 years as too far ahead to plan.

Business failure is often linked to a lack of strategic financial planning and control. Managers look at several linked factors, remembered by the word SPACED.

Business Management Toolkit: culture and sources of finance

Culture affects which sources are used and available. Family and friends are not named in the syllabus as a source of finance, but they are popular in many cultures, especially for sole traders and partnerships. For example, in India small business owners often raise large sums from close family and friends.

  • Advantage: usually fairly easy and cheaper than a bank, which may ask for collateral.
  • Disadvantage: very limited amounts, and borrowing from family and friends often leads to conflict and disputes, in any culture.

Sources of finance and the key concepts

  • All sources have the same purpose: to fund business activity.
  • Serious cash flow problems (being unable to pay short term debts) can cause liquidation or bankruptcy. This is worse if creditors chase debtors for money owed and the firm cannot repay straight away.
  • Any good business strategy must plan the sources of finance so there is enough to run the organization sustainably.
  • Finance needs change as a business grows and evolves.
  • Ethics: the choice and control of finance affects financial performance. Financial accounts (profit and loss account and balance sheet, see 3.4) must be reported legally and ethically (in a transparent way).

Cost and price are not the same

Definition

Cost refers to the sum of money incurred by a business in the production process, such as the costs of raw materials, wages and salaries, insurance, advertising and rent.

Definition

Price refers to the amount of money a product is sold for. It is the sum paid by the customer to purchase a good or service.

Exam tip

Remember that cost is the cost of production, paid by the producer. Price is the amount the product is sold for, paid by the customer. In everyday talk people say a shirt "cost $25". In business language, the price is $25.

Types of cost in the syllabus

(i) Fixed costs

Definition

Fixed costs are the costs that do not vary with the level of output. They exist even if there is no output.

  • A business must pay them regardless of how much it produces or sells. They are independent of output or sales.
  • Examples: rent on leased premises, interest on bank loans, advertising, market research, management salaries, office stationery, security and professional accountancy fees.
  • Fixed costs can change, but not because of output. For example, a landlord raises the rent, or directors get higher salaries because of rising living costs.
  • On a graph, total fixed costs (TFC) are a horizontal line.

(ii) Variable costs

Definition

Variable costs are costs of production that change in proportion to the level of output, such as raw materials and hourly wages of production workers.

  • If output or sales double, variable costs also double.
  • In theory, with no production, variable costs should be zero.
  • Examples: raw materials (for a firm making curtains), sales staff commission, hourly wages of production workers, packaging tied to output.
  • On a graph, total variable costs (TVC) are a line that starts at zero and rises as output rises.

Total costs and average cost

Definition

Total costs are the sum of all variable costs and all fixed costs of production. Total costs (TC) = total variable costs (TVC) + total fixed costs (TFC).

Definition

Average cost (AC) refers to the cost per unit of output. It is calculated as AC = TC ÷ Q, where TC is total cost and Q is quantity (or output level).

Worked example

A bakery has total fixed costs of $500 a month and variable costs of $1.20 per loaf. It bakes 400 loaves.

Step 1: TVC = $1.20 × 400 = $480.
Step 2: TC = TVC + TFC = $480 + $500 = $980.
Step 3: AC = TC ÷ Q = $980 ÷ 400 = $2.45 per loaf.
If output rises to 800 loaves, TVC doubles to $960 but TFC stays at $500. TC = $1,460 and AC falls to $1.83 (rounded), because the fixed costs are spread over more units.

Common mistake

Avoid circular answers: do not repeat the question in your answer. When asked to distinguish fixed and variable costs, do not write "fixed costs are costs that are fixed and variable costs are costs that are variable". Say that fixed costs do not change with output, and variable costs change in proportion to output. Review any key term you cannot define clearly in your own words.

(iii) Direct costs

Definition

Direct costs are costs specifically attributed to the production or sale of a particular good or service.

  • Related to an individual project or the output of a particular product. Without it, the cost would not exist.
  • Direct costs can include variable costs such as raw materials. But unlike variable costs, direct costs are not necessarily linked to the level of output. They can be fixed costs.
  • Example: the direct costs of buying a commercial building include consultancy costs, solicitor's fees, telephone bills, postage, photocopying and bank charges.
  • Direct costs can be traced back to the output of a product or to a specific cost centre.

What is variable for one business is not always variable for another. Catering on a flight with a mainstream airline such as Singapore Airlines is a variable cost, because more passengers need more food and drink, which are included in the ticket price. For a no frills airline such as Ryanair, easyJet or AirAsia, catering is a direct cost. It relates to the flight, but not every passenger buys meals, because they are paid for individually.

(iv) Indirect costs (overheads)

Definition

Indirect costs (or overheads) are costs that do not directly relate to the production or sale of a specific product.

  • They cannot be clearly traced to the production or sale of any single product. Example: rent and lighting serve all areas of the business.
  • Examples: advertising, legal expenses, salaries of administrative staff, insurance premiums, security, office stationery, shipping and postage, utility bills, accounting fees.
  • Indirect costs can be fixed costs, since they do not link directly to output. But unlike fixed costs, they are not easily identified with a particular activity or product.

Exam tip

The terms direct and indirect costs are used for businesses that make or sell a range of products and have cost centres and profit centres. These costs can be fixed or variable, depending on the business. The terms fixed and variable costs, as used in break even analysis, are used when a business sells one type of product.

Revenue

Definition

Revenue is the money that a business earns from the sale of goods and services. It is calculated by multiplying the unit price of each product by the quantity sold.

Definition

Total revenue refers to the money coming into a business, usually from the sale of goods and/or services. It is calculated by multiplying the price of a product with the quantity sold.

Definition

Average revenue (AR) refers to the value of sales received from customers per unit of a good or service sold. It is calculated as AR = TR ÷ Q = P, where TR is total revenue.

Definition

Profit exists if there is a positive difference between a firm's total revenues and its total costs.

Formula: Sales revenue = Price × Quantity sold (also written TR = P × Q). Note: the textbook says the revenue formulae are not given to candidates in the exam, so learn them.

Worked example

Church's, an English maker of luxury shoes, charges an average of $300 a pair and sells 3,000 pairs in a week.

Sales revenue = Price × Quantity sold = $300 × 3,000 = $900,000 for that week.
AR = TR ÷ Q = $900,000 ÷ 3,000 = $300, which is the same as the price.
If total costs that week were $700,000, profit = $900,000 minus $700,000 = $200,000.

About Church's: founded in 1873 by Thomas Church and his three sons, main operations in Northampton, UK. It can make up to 5,000 pairs a week, with about 70% exported. It has shops in New York, Milan, Paris, Rome, Madrid, Hong Kong, Singapore and Shanghai. It has been owned by Prada since 1999, in a deal worth $170 million.

Definition

A revenue stream refers to the money coming into a business from its various business activities, such as sponsorship deals, merchandise and receipt of royalty payments.

Revenue does not only come from selling goods and services. The mix of revenue streams depends on the type of firm and its activities. For example, the IB itself earns from: annual fees from IB World Schools, authorization and evaluation fees, donations, examination fees, IB global and regional conferences, royalties from publications and workshops (training events for teachers).

Costs, revenues and the key concepts

  • Knowing costs lets managers stay in control of finances, especially in times of change and uncertainty. Cost control is crucial to keep or improve profitability.
  • Large businesses benefit from measuring the financial performance of each division. They use cost and profit centres, budgets and variance analysis (later chapters).
  • Cost cutting does not automatically raise profitability, but it helps to keep costs under control.
  • How much a business focuses on cost control depends on its organizational culture and management preferences.
  • Cost cutting can be justified if it leads to sustained efficiency and profitability.
  • Ethics: problems arise if cost cutting harms staff, for example poorer working conditions, limited training, pay cuts or job losses.
  • Raising revenue is just as important, if not more so. The UK Institute of Management suggests successful businesses spend about 80% of their strategy on raising revenue and only 20% on cutting costs. Forward looking, creative businesses can spend more if revenue and profit allow it. Example: Kering bought Gucci and Yves Saint Laurent to earn more revenue, not to save costs.
  • Some businesses use the FORCE approach (Focus On Reducing Costs Everywhere) to stay competitive.
  • Business Management Toolkit: contribution analysis (later chapter) can help businesses control costs better.

The purpose of final accounts

Definition

Final accounts are the published annual financial statements that all limited liability companies are legally obliged to report, namely the balance sheet and the P&L account.

  • All businesses need to keep financial records. Good accounts give better financial control and planning, and may be a legal requirement.
  • Where ownership and control are separate (see Unit 1), much of the finance may come from shareholders. Financial reporting accounts for the money of the business, whether it belongs to owners, investors or lenders.
  • Final accounts make sure all payments and receipts are officially accounted for, and give transparency in the use of a company's funds.
  • Most countries require companies to have their final accounts audited by independent, chartered accountants, who certify that they are accurate and truthful.
  • There is no universal format. Companies have some flexibility, as long as they follow the rules of their country.
  • Internal stakeholders (executives, directors) use accounts to manage the business and make strategic decisions. For external stakeholders the purpose is to support evaluative judgements, such as whether the firm can pay suppliers or repay lenders.

Real world example

Enron. A huge US energy company with sales of over $100 billion and over 20,000 staff. The profit and loss account

Definition

The profit and loss (P&L) account (or income statement) is a financial statement of a firm's trading activities over a period of time, usually one year. It shows the revenues and expenses of a business at the end of a set accounting period, and so the value of profit or loss.

  • Revenues are inflows of money from ordinary trading: cash sales, credit sales, fees and royalties.
  • Costs are outflows of money from operations: wages, salaries, rents and purchases of stock (inventories).
  • Profit gives most businesses an incentive to do well. Even non profit and public sector bodies need a surplus to survive.
  • There are three sections: (i) the trading account, (ii) the profit and loss account and (iii) the appropriation account.

(i) The trading account: gross profit

Definition

Gross profit is the difference between the sales revenue of a business and its direct costs incurred in making or purchasing the products that have been sold to its customers.

Definition

Cost of goods sold (COGS), also known as cost of sales (COS), refers to the direct costs of producing or purchasing stock that has been sold to customers.

Formulae

Gross profit = Sales revenue minus Cost of goods sold

COGS = Opening stock + Purchases minus Closing stock

Worked example (from the textbook)

A business opens with $5,000 of stock (at cost). It buys $25,000 more. At the end it has $3,800 of stock left. It sells stock for three times its cost.

Step 1: COGS = $5,000 + $25,000 minus $3,800 = $26,200.
Step 2: Sales revenue = $26,200 × 3 = $78,600.
Step 3: Gross profit = $78,600 minus $26,200 = $52,400.

(ii) The profit and loss account: profit

Definition

Profit is the surplus (if any) that a business earns after all expenses have been paid for from the firm's gross profit.

Definition

Expenses are the indirect or fixed costs of production, such as administration charges, management salaries, insurance premiums and rent.

Formula

Net profit = Gross profit minus Expenses

Net profit is the actual profit earned from the firm's normal trading activities. Negative numbers (costs and expenses) are shown in brackets.

How a business might cut expenses to raise profit

  • Rent: negotiate lower rent, or move to cheaper premises (relocation may be hard because of industrial inertia).
  • Fuel: use less heating, air conditioning and lighting. This lowers bills and helps the environment.
  • Administration: review clerical work, combine jobs, employ fewer people or outsource some jobs.

Interest and tax

  • Interest and tax are expenses, but they are shown separately. This is because interest rates and tax rates change over time and are outside the firm's control. Profit may be lower this year only because rates rose.
  • Showing profit before interest and tax lets firms compare like with like and compare over time.
  • Interest is the charge or fee paid to financiers such as banks. Corporate tax is the levy payable on a company's profits.

Example: Florists R Us pays $10,000 interest and corporate tax of 10%. Profit before interest and tax is $65,000. Minus interest $10,000 = profit before tax $55,000. Tax = 10% × $55,000 = $5,500. Profit after interest and tax = $49,500.

(iii) The appropriation account: dividends and retained profit

This is the final section. It shows how the net profit after interest and tax is shared out.

  • Dividends: the part paid to the owners (shareholders). The proportion is decided by the Board of Directors and approved at the Annual General Meeting. Dividends are usually paid twice a year: an interim dividend about halfway through the year and a final dividend at the end of the fiscal year.
  • Retained profit: how much of the profit after interest and tax the business keeps for its own use, as an internal source of finance, for example to reinvest or expand. For non profit organizations the term is retained surplus. It is transferred to the Equity section of the balance sheet as retained earnings.
Definition

Retained profit is the amount of profit after interest, tax and dividends have been paid. It is then reinvested in the business for its own use.

Exam tip

You will not be asked about the trading account, profit statement and appropriation account separately. Questions use the P&L account in its entirety. Limitations of the P&L account

  • It shows historical performance. Past success does not guarantee future performance.
  • There is no international standard format, so comparing firms in different countries or industries is hard.
  • Window dressing can occur (see below). For example, including the sale of fixed assets or non operating income to boost profit and impress shareholders.
Definition

Window dressing refers to the legal act of creative accounting by manipulating financial data to make the results appear more appealing.

The P&L account is still important because it shows the profit or loss after all costs. If expenses are higher than gross profit, the business makes an overall loss and cannot survive for long.

The balance sheet

Definition

A balance sheet (or statement of financial position) contains financial information about an organization's assets, liabilities and the capital invested by the owners, showing a snapshot of the firm's financial situation.

  • All limited liability companies must produce one for auditing.
  • It shows the position on one day only, usually the last day of the accounting year.
  • It "balances" because it shows where the money came from (sources of finance, shown as equity) and what it was spent on (net assets).
  • Example: a building has a market value of $550,000 and an outstanding mortgage of $350,000. The $200,000 difference is the firm's equity in the property.
  • Established businesses often report two years of figures (a legal requirement in many countries) so stakeholders can compare.
  • It has three parts: (i) assets, (ii) liabilities, (iii) equity.

(i) Assets

Definition

Assets are items of monetary value that are owned by a business, such as cash, stocks and property (premises).

Definition

Noncurrent assets are items owned by a business, not intended for sale within the next twelve months, but used repeatedly to generate revenue, such as property, plant and equipment.

Definition

A current asset refers to cash or any other liquid asset that is likely to be turned into cash within 12 months of the balance sheet date. Examples include cash, debtors and stocks.

Current assetMeaning
CashMoney held in the business or at the bank.
DebtorsPeople or organizations that owe money to the business because they bought on credit. It is a current asset because the money belongs to the business.
StocksUnsold raw materials, semi finished goods (work in progress) and finished goods. Finished stocks are more liquid than raw materials.
Exam tip

An asset belongs to a business, but that does not mean it has been paid for. A building worth millions may be mostly funded by debt (a mortgage), with the rest being equity.

(ii) Liabilities

Definition

A liability is a legal obligation of a business to repay its lenders or suppliers at a later date. Liabilities are the amount of money owed by the business.

Definition

Noncurrent liabilities (long term liabilities) are the debts owed by a business that are expected to take longer than a year from the balance sheet date to repay. Examples: mortgages, bank loans, debentures.

Definition

Current liabilities are debts that must be settled within one year of the balance sheet date. Examples include bank overdrafts, trade creditors and other short term loans.

  • Bank overdrafts: must be repaid quickly because the interest rate tends to be very high.
  • Trade creditors: suppliers who must be repaid for goods bought on trade credit, usually within 30 to 60 days.
  • Other short term loans: loans that fall due within 12 months.

Net assets and (iii) equity

Definition

Net assets show the value of a business to its owners by calculating the value of all its assets minus its liabilities. This figure must match the equity of the business in the balance sheet.

Formulae

Net assets = (Noncurrent assets + Current assets) minus (Noncurrent liabilities + Current liabilities)

or Net assets = Total assets minus Total liabilities

So: Total assets minus Total liabilities = Net assets = Total equity

Definition

Equity (also called capital and reserves) refers to the value of the business that belongs to the owners. For limited liability companies, it is made up of share capital and retained earnings. For sole traders and partnerships, only retained earnings are recorded, because there are no shareholders.

Definition

Share capital refers to the amount of money raised through the sale of shares. It shows the value raised when the shares were first sold, rather than their current market value.

  • Retained earnings are the total accumulated profit after interest, tax and dividends have been paid, reinvested for the business's own use. They belong to the owners, so they appear under owners' equity or shareholders' equity. The figures come from the P&L accounts.
Exam tip

Retained profit appears in both the P&L account and the balance sheet. In the P&L it shows the profit after interest and tax kept in the business instead of paid as dividends. In the balance sheet it appears in the equity section as retained earnings, because it is an internal source of finance.

  • Their balance sheets differ slightly from those of limited liability companies.
  • They cannot sell shares, so there is no share capital. Money put in by owners is recorded as owners' equity or owners' capital.
  • There are no dividends. Instead the sole trader or partners may take money out for personal use, called drawings.
  • Limited companies can sell shares and debentures (debentures are recorded under long term liabilities). Neither appears on the balance sheet of an unincorporated business.

The main difference between the balance sheets of incorporated and unincorporated businesses is that unincorporated ones have no share capital in equity. Most IB questions ask you to interpret the balance sheet of a limited liability company. Use the format in Table 17.3 and add a title.

Limitations of balance sheets

  • Static: it shows one day only. The position can change the next day, for example when funds are used for expansion.
  • Figures are only estimates. The market value of an asset is not the same as its book value. The true market value of a noncurrent asset is only known when it is sold. The statement also lacks a detailed breakdown of assets.
  • No universal format, so comparing firms (even in one industry) is hard.
  • Not all assets are included, especially intangible assets and human capital. A sports club does not put its players or its manager on the balance sheet, even though they have a market value. If employees really are a firm's most valuable asset, the balance sheet does not show the firm's true position.
Definition

Book value is the value of an asset as shown on a balance sheet. The market value of assets can be higher than its book value because of intangible assets such as the brand value or goodwill.

Intangible assets

Definition

Intangible assets are noncurrent assets that do not exist in a physical form but are of monetary value, such as goodwill, copyrights, brand names and registered trademarks.

  • They are non physical fixed assets that can earn revenue. They are protected by laws called intellectual property rights (IPR).
  • They can be a large part of a firm's value, but it is hard to put an objective and accurate price on them.
  • The main types: (i) branding, (ii) patents, (iii) copyrights, (iv) goodwill, (v) registered trademarks.

Goodwill is an intangible asset which exists when the value of a firm exceeds its book value (the value of the firm's net assets).

Valuing intangible assets

  • Intangible assets are not always recorded on the balance sheet because their value is so hard to measure objectively. No two firms would value the Google brand, or the goodwill of employees in a school, in the same way.
  • Goodwill is only truly realised when a takeover happens and the buyer pays a premium. That premium is the goodwill: the difference between the value of the business and the purchase price.
  • Adding intangible assets can be a form of window dressing to inflate a firm's value. This is legal but can distort the true health of the business. Example: in 2006 Vodafone turned an €11 billion loss into a €10 billion profit by legally including goodwill.
  • Intangible assets do add value beyond book value. The problem is the uncertainty over how to value them.
Real world examples

Google's brand value. In 2014 Forbes estimated it at about $47bn, but Interbrand estimated over $93bn. This large gap shows how subjective brand values are. In early 2020 Google became the third US company worth $1 trillion by market value (after Apple and Microsoft).

Harry Potter. J.K. Rowling wrote the first book in 1994. Warner Bros bought the film rights in 1998, and "Harry Potter World" was announced in 2007. Her books have sold over 400 million copies in over 65 languages. TIME values the Harry Potter brand at over $25bn, so Rowling, her publishers and Warner Bros protect their intangible assets, including the international copyrights.

Limitations of final accounts

  • One year of accounts alone is of little use. A series of accounts shows trends.
  • You also need other firms' accounts to benchmark against competitors, which takes time.
  • Human resources are ignored: skills, loyalty, goodwill and motivation of employees. Failing to keep or motivate staff can hurt future finances.
  • They show nothing about non financial matters such as organizational culture and contribution to local communities. Qualitative factors matter in decisions too, for example ethical objectives and location.
  • Accounts must be produced lawfully, but they do not reveal the whole truth. Firms limit (within reason) what they disclose because the accounts are public and competitors can see them.
  • They are historical and show one point in time. Past performance is not a guide to current or future performance.

Depreciation (Higher Level only)

Definition

Depreciation is the fall in the value of noncurrent assets over time, caused by wear and tear (due to the asset being used) or obsolescence (out dated).

  • The opposite is appreciation: a rise in the value of a noncurrent asset. Land and property tend to rise in value, but most noncurrent assets depreciate.
  • Wear and tear: computers and vehicles wear out, maintenance costs rise and value falls.
  • Obsolescence: newer, better products make older assets (old vehicles, computers, smartphones, software) less valuable.
  • Depreciation spreads the historic cost of a fixed asset over its useful life. The fall in value shows on the balance sheet as accumulated depreciation.

Why depreciation is recorded

  • To calculate the value of the business more accurately. Depreciating assets lowers net assets to a truer value. Revaluing assets that have appreciated raises it.
  • To assess the value of noncurrent assets realistically over time, since the historic cost is unlikely to equal today's market value.
  • To plan for replacing assets. Depreciation is also recorded in the P&L account as an expense, because the asset will eventually need to be replaced.
Definition

The historic cost refers to the purchase cost of a particular fixed asset. It is used in the calculation of depreciation.

Definition

The residual value (or scrap value) is an estimate of the value of the noncurrent asset at the end of its useful life.

The IB syllabus names two methods. Which one is used matters less than being consistent, so that comparisons over time are meaningful.

(i) Straight line method

Definition

The straight line method is a means of calculating depreciation that reduces the value of a fixed asset by the same value each year throughout its useful life.

Formula

Annual depreciation = Purchase cost ÷ Lifespan

With a residual value: Annual depreciation = (Purchase cost minus Residual value) ÷ Lifespan

  • Three variables: the life expectancy of the asset, the scrap (residual) value and the historic cost.
  • It is the easier method and the most commonly used.
  • Many firms use a zero residual value because estimates can be very inaccurate. But most fixed assets can still fetch a small price when sold.
Worked example (from the textbook)

An electronic security system costs $25,000 and is expected to last 5 years.

Annual depreciation = $25,000 ÷ 5 = $5,000 a year.
If it can be sold for $5,000 after 5 years: ($25,000 minus $5,000) ÷ 5 = $4,000 a year, which is $1,000 a year less than with no residual value.

(ii) Units of production method

Definition

The units of production method of calculating depreciation allocates an equal amount of depreciation to each unit of output rendered by a noncurrent asset.

It uses physical output, so it fits assets whose fall in value depends on usage rather than time. For example, vehicles depreciate with more miles. The same applies to photocopiers, printers and other machinery. It is also called the units of output method.

Formulae (two steps)

Step 1: Depreciation per unit = (Purchase cost minus Scrap value) ÷ Expected number of units over lifetime

Step 2: Depreciation expense = Depreciation per unit × Number of units produced

Worked example (from the textbook)

A printing machine costs $500,000. It is expected to last 5,000 hours over 5 years, and then be sold for $100,000.

Depreciation per hour = ($500,000 minus $100,000) ÷ 5,000 = $80 per hour.
The machine runs 1,500 hours in year 1: depreciation expense = $80 × 1,500 = $120,000.
This reduction in value is shown as accumulated depreciation (a negative amount) on the balance sheet.

Worked example (extra practice)

A delivery van costs $40,000, has a scrap value of $10,000 and is expected to drive 300,000 km. In year 1 it drives 45,000 km.

Depreciation per km = ($40,000 minus $10,000) ÷ 300,000 = $0.10 per km.
Year 1 expense = $0.10 × 45,000 = $4,500. Book value at end of year 1 = $40,000 minus $4,500 = $35,500.

This method is more cumbersome than straight line, because the depreciation must be worked out for each period from the usage rate. But the extra effort gives better insight into the true running cost of machinery and other capital equipment, and shows how fast the asset's value is being used up. This can help firms that seek external funding for growth.

Worked example (straight line, with book value)

A machine costs $60,000, has a residual value of $6,000 and a life of 6 years.

Annual depreciation = ($60,000 minus $6,000) ÷ 6 = $9,000.
Accumulated depreciation after 2 years = 2 × $9,000 = $18,000. Book value = $60,000 minus $18,000 = $42,000.

  • Straight line suits: assets that lose value fairly evenly over time or are not used heavily (buildings, office furniture), small assets, and firms that want a simple method.
  • Units of production suits: assets whose value falls mainly with use, and where use varies a lot from year to year (machines in a factory, vehicles), and where an accurate asset value matters for pricing or funding decisions.

Final accounts and the key concepts

  • Final accounts give disclosure and accountability. Used properly and ethically, they show the financial health and profitability of a business and help with strategic planning and change management.
  • If profit comes from a one off sale of noncurrent assets (land or property), profitability is probably low. Window dressing is not illegal but raises ethical questions.
  • The balance sheet shows the liquidity position, which is the ability to meet daily running costs. Both statements are used to secure external funding for growth and sustainability.
  • Intellectual property rights (IPRs) such as patents and copyrights are noncurrent assets. They can act as a barrier to entry, protecting market share. Coca Cola, Toyota and Disney use IPRs this way. Many firms now see IPRs as an offensive strategy, not just a defensive one.
  • Creativity and new product development matter for long term survival. Legal protection of new products, designs and processes can raise the firm's value on its balance sheet.
  • Ethics: different countries have different laws and cultural norms about final accounts, but all business decisions, including financial reporting, have ethical effects on stakeholders. Wrongdoing, even if not illegal, is unethical. Professional bodies monitor ethical standards, for example the Australian Accounting Standards Board, the Accounting Standards Committee of Germany and America's Financial Accounting Standards Board. Accountants must be open, transparent and honest. Most businesses have an annual audit to check integrity.
Exam tip

Look beyond the financial data. Final accounts are only one part of the quantitative analysis. Budgeting matters for daily operations and investment appraisal for growth plans. Also consider qualitative issues such as objectives, the state of the economy and corporate culture. Treat any single statement with some caution.

Key terms
  • Final accounts: the published annual statements (P&L account and balance sheet).
  • P&L account: statement of revenues, costs and profit over a period.
  • Cost of goods sold (cost of sales): direct costs of the stock actually sold.
  • Gross profit: sales revenue minus cost of goods sold.
  • Expenses: indirect or fixed costs such as rent and salaries.
  • Profit: surplus after all expenses are paid from gross profit.
  • Retained profit: profit after interest, tax and dividends, kept in the business.
  • Window dressing: legal creative accounting to make results look better.
  • Balance sheet: snapshot of assets, liabilities and owners' capital.
  • Noncurrent / current asset: lasts over 12 months / turns to cash within 12 months.
  • Noncurrent / current liability: repaid after / within 12 months.
  • Net assets: total assets minus total liabilities, equal to equity.
  • Equity: share capital plus retained earnings.
  • Share capital: money raised by first selling shares.
  • Creditors: suppliers owed money. Debtors: customers who owe money.
  • Book value: the value of an asset on the balance sheet.
  • Intangible assets: non physical noncurrent assets such as goodwill, brands, patents, copyrights and trademarks.
  • Goodwill: when a firm's value exceeds its book value.
  • Depreciation: fall in the value of noncurrent assets over time.
  • Historic cost, residual value: purchase cost, and estimated value at the end of its life.
  • Straight line / units of production: equal depreciation each year / equal depreciation per unit of output.

Quick check

1. A firm has total assets of $900,000 and total liabilities of $400,000. Share capital is $350,000. Find net assets and retained earnings.

Net assets = $900,000 minus $400,000 = $500,000. Equity must equal net assets, so retained earnings = $500,000 minus $350,000 = $150,000.

2. A machine costs $30,000, has a scrap value of $3,000 and lasts 9 years. Find the annual straight line depreciation.

($30,000 minus $3,000) ÷ 9 = $3,000 a year.

3. State two limitations of the balance sheet.

Any two of: it is a snapshot of one day only, figures are estimates and book value may differ from market value, there is no universal format, intangible assets and human capital are often left out.

4. Why do banks and suppliers read a firm's final accounts?

Banks (financiers) check whether the firm can repay loans. Suppliers check whether it is stable enough to be given trade credit and to pay on time.
